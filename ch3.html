This chapter sets out the aerodynamic model on which the crew's trim and steering decisions rest. It begins with the geometry of apparent wind, because at skiff speeds the wind the sails meet is dominated by the boat's own motion, and every later idea is expressed in apparent-wind terms. It then treats the sail as a lifting surface with an effective angle of attack, and the mainsail and jib as a single slot system rather than two sails. Twist and loading follow, because , heel and sea state change the angle of attack along the span faster than any single control can follow. A short section on the gennaker covers and luff behaviour at high apparent-wind speeds. The coupling section connects the sail-force vector to heel, leeway and foil loading, so that depowering is understood as a hydrodynamic decision as much as an aerodynamic one. The final section defines the process by which trim knowledge is measured, benchmarked and reduced to on-water cues.

3.1Apparent-wind geometry at skiff speeds

Apparent wind is "the of the TRUE WIND and the BOAT COURSE" S19; it is the only wind the sails, the telltales and the crew experience. In a 49er, boat speed is a large fraction of true-wind speed on every leg, so the apparent wind is always well forward of the true wind and often stronger than it. Most of what the crew perceives as wind behaviour is therefore a mixture of true-wind change and boat-speed change, and the purpose of this section is to separate the two and to keep the resultant stable.

3.1.1Apparent-wind angle as the primary steering-and-trim reference. The sails generate force as a function of their angle to the apparent wind, not to the true wind, so the apparent-wind angle is the reference against which sheet and course are set. Upwind the helm steers to the jib luff telltales, a direct reading of the local apparent-wind angle at the entry. Downwind the reference is the gennaker luff, and Ingham's observation that on an asymmetric boat "The apparent-wind angle basically remains at 90 degrees" S27S28 states the target: trim and steering hold that angle nearly constant while boat speed, not course, absorbs changes in true wind. Compass heading is a secondary record for shift detection, not the primary trim input.

3.1.2Apparent-wind velocity changes through gusts, lulls, waves, and acceleration. Apparent-wind speed rises with true-wind speed and with boat speed, because as boat speed increases "the apparent wind speed increases and the apparent wind angle decreases, moving further forward" S26. A gust therefore arrives twice: first as a jump in true wind, which raises apparent speed and moves it aft, then as an acceleration, which raises apparent speed again and brings the angle forward. A lull reverses both stages. A wave impact removes boat speed, so the apparent wind drops and rotates aft at the instant the hull needs power to re-accelerate. Bethwaite's measurements, with wind speed rising and falling by about 7 percent every 6 to 12 seconds S50, set the time scale on which these cycles run.

3.1.3Separating true-wind shifts from changes created by boat speed. A change in apparent-wind angle has two candidate causes, a true-wind shift or a boat-speed change, and the correct response differs. The test is the pairing of angle with speed. When the apparent wind moves aft while the boat is slowing, or forward while it is accelerating, the cause is boat speed, and the response is to hold the course and let trim follow the speed. When the angle moves with no matching speed change, or moves the opposite way to what the speed change predicts, the cause is a true shift, and the helm steers to it. The compass and the speed record confirm afterwards: once the helm has steered to it, a true shift shows as a changed heading at the same apparent angle with no matching speed change, whereas a speed change shows in the alone, at constant heading.

3.1.4Preserving apparent-wind stability during transitions between modes. A mode change, pointing to fast upwind or high road to low road downwind, changes boat speed and therefore rotates the apparent wind. If the sails are re-trimmed to the new course before the speed has changed, the angle of attack is wrong during the transition, and the boat loses the speed the new mode depends on. Ingham's rule that "Speed before depth is essential because once the boat is going well, the apparent wind goes forward and the boat can go lower" S27S28 is the general form: speed first, then course, with trim eased or firmed progressively so that the apparent-wind angle at the luff stays inside its narrow target band throughout.

3.2Lift, drag, and effective angle of attack

The force of the wind on a sail "is calculated as a perpendicular to the apparent wind direction and a drag force in the direction of the apparent wind" S19. Both depend on the angle at which the sail meets the flow and on how the flow behaves over the surface. The angle that matters is the effective angle of attack: and the sail's after every distortion of the flow field has been included. The section's argument is that the crew manages this angle, not the sail's appearance.

3.2.3Managing effective angle of attack rather than visual sail shape alone. Gladstone's are angle of attack, depth and twist S20, and the three are not independent: depth sets the camber and twist sets how the angle of attack varies with height. A sail that looks correct from the boom may be operating at the wrong effective angle, because heel, leeway, the from the jib and the all alter the flow the cloth actually meets. The management rule is to trim on flow evidence, telltales, leech ribbons and the pressure felt through the sheet and the tiller, and to use the visual shape as a record for repeatability rather than as the target.

3.2.4 , separation onset, and stalled-sail recognition. Attached flow follows the curvature of both surfaces from luff to leech. As the effective angle of attack rises, the leeward flow first thickens and then detaches near the leech, and the detached region grows forward until the sail is stalled, lift collapses and drag remains high. The onset is read from the leeward telltales: the aft one lifts and stirs first, then the forward ones stall. On the water a stalled mainsail feels heavy on the sheet but produces no acceleration, the boat heels without speed and the helm loads up. A stalled jib shows its leeward telltales hanging and the boat sagging to leeward despite a full-looking sail.

3.2.5Restoring attached flow through sheet tension, vang, and steering. Recovery reduces the effective angle of attack, and each control does it differently. Easing the sheet opens the leech and reduces the angle, from the head first, while the foot deepens. so that the upper sail unloads while the boom position holds. Easing the sheet with the vang loaded reduces the angle along the whole span at constant twist. Steering up reduces the angle across both sails at once and is the fastest tool, but it spends the speed that stalling has already reduced. The decision rule is to use the control that acts where the stall began: vang or sheet for a stall from the head, sheet against a loaded vang or helm for a stall along the whole span.

3.3Mainsail–jib interaction

The mainsail and jib form one aerodynamic system. The jib sits in the ahead of the mainsail's leading edge, and the mainsail sits in the downwash of the jib, so each modifies the flow the other meets. The gap between them, , is not a nozzle that speeds the air but a region whose width and shape decide whether the mainsail's leeward flow stays attached. Trim decisions are therefore made on the pair, and a change to one sail is evaluated by its effect on the other.

3.3.1Slot geometry as a system rather than two independently trimmed sails. The mainsail's presence turns the flow ahead of it, so the jib operates at a higher effective angle of attack than its setting alone suggests and carries more of the total lift. The jib's presence turns the flow at the mainsail's luff, reducing the mainsail's effective angle there and letting it hold at a sheeting angle that would stall it alone. The slot is the geometric expression of this exchange. Its width must stay proportionate: a slot that narrows aloft chokes the mainsail's upper leeward flow, and one that opens too far aloft wastes the jib's upper span. Trim is judged by the pair's combined flow, not by either sail's appearance.

3.3.2Jib entry angle and its effect on mainsail attachment. The jib's entry angle, the angle of its forward section to the apparent wind, sets the helm's tolerance for steering error and, through the downwash, the angle at which the mainsail's luff meets the flow. A finer entry gives higher pointing potential and a narrow groove; a rounder entry gives a wider groove and forgiveness in waves. Headstay sag makes the jib fuller S33, and in the 49er the sag is governed by rig tension, so entry is a rig setting as much as a sheet setting. The class guide's baseline cap shroud tension up to about 11 to 12 knots, tighter above S13, is the coarse entry control; the crew's own tension per band is crew value.

3.3.3 Mainsail leech position and behind the jib. Air leaving the jib leech has been accelerated over the jib's leeward side and must slow again toward free-stream pressure before it reaches the mainsail leech. The mainsail's leech position controls how gently that recovery happens. A leech carried too far to windward, from oversheeting or too little twist, steepens the pressure rise and the flow separates from the mainsail's after part; a leech too open lets the jib's flow escape without adding to the mainsail's lift. Dellenbaugh's reference, mainsheet trimmed "until the top batten is parallel to the boom" S34, is the starting point from which the crew opens or closes the leech according to leech-ribbon behaviour and the helm load the boat reports.

3.3.4Balancing jib-sheet tension, jib-leech tension, and mainsail twist. Three controls set the slot's shape along its height. Jib-sheet tension sets the foot and the lower slot. Jib-leech tension, governed by the lead position, which the class tuning guide sets with the jib sheet in the middle hole and the track pins in the innermost holes for winds up to about 12 knots and flat water S13, sets the upper slot. Mainsail twist sets the leeward wall of the slot. The balance rule is that jib twist and mainsail twist change together: when the vang or sheet opens the mainsail's upper leech, the jib's upper leech opens with it, otherwise the slot narrows aloft at the moment the mainsail is trying to shed load there.

3.3.5Slot response in flat water, chop, marginal trapezing, and full-power conditions. In flat water the slot is closed to its narrowest working width, because steering error is small and the boat can hold a fine effective angle. In chop swings the apparent wind at the luff, so the slot is opened, the entry rounded and the twist increased to widen the groove. In marginal trapezing conditions the priority is power, so depth is added, twist reduced and the slot held as closed as attached flow allows, since "More depth creates more power and better acceleration" S20. At full power the slot is opened from the top through twist, keeping the lower sails driving while the upper sails shed the load the crew cannot hold.

3.3.6Coordinated helm and crew trim language for repeatable changes. Because each sail changes the other, the helm and crew adjust in an agreed sequence with a shared vocabulary. Each call names the control, the direction and the size of the step in the crew's own units, for example a fixed length of jib sheet or a marked vang position, so that a change made in a race can be reproduced in training. The order of operations is fixed: the twist controls move before the sheeting angles, and the jib follows the mainsail. The reference marks on sheets and vang are calibrated at the start of each block, and any change that cannot be stated in this language is treated as unrecorded and not carried forward.

3.3.7Gold-medal standard: know reference, fast-mode, and pointing-mode trim in every major wind range. An observer holding the crew's trim sheet can, in any wind band the crew races in, watch the boat set up and read off three states: the reference trim, the fast-mode variant and the pointing-mode variant, each with its jib-sheet mark, jib-lead position, mainsheet mark, vang mark, cunningham and outhaul settings recorded as crew value. The crew moves between the three states in an agreed number of moves, and the transition is complete before the boat has sailed a stated distance. The speed and heading traces from the logger show the expected change, and the observer cannot find a band in which the crew is improvising.

3.4Spanwise loading and twist management

Twist distributes lift , from foot to head, and matches the sail's angle of attack to a wind that changes in speed and direction with height. Twist is needed because , which gives "a stronger apparent wind and a wider apparent wind angle aloft" S21. The two twist controls, vang and sheet, act differently on the mainsail, and this section separates them so that the crew can choose the one that changes only what needs changing.

3.4.1Twist as a control of load distribution from foot to head. Gladstone's rule, "Increasing twist reduces power; decreasing twist adds power" S20, describes the aggregate, but the mechanism is spanwise. Lift produced high on the sail heels the boat through a long lever and drives it through the same force as lift produced low, which heels through a short lever. Twist moves lift down the span by reducing the angle of attack of the upper sections, so that at a given total heeling moment the boat carries more drive. Reducing twist loads the head and raises power and heeling moment together. The crew therefore treats twist first as the control that decides where along the span the sail works, and only secondarily as a total-power control.

3.4.2Matching twist to wind gradient, heel angle, and sea state. Base twist follows the gradient. Gilbert's figures for a model-yacht rig, about 4 degrees of twist close-hauled and about 12 degrees with the wind abeam S22, give the relationship's direction for any rig: required twist rises as the wind angle opens, because the apparent-wind angle aloft diverges further from that at deck level. Heel adds a second term, since a heeled rig presents its upper sections at a different angle from its lower ones; skiff coaching guidance treats flat as flat, plus or minus 5 degrees acceptable S36. Sea state adds a third: at the head more than at the foot, so twist is increased in waves to keep the head attached.

3.4.3Leech stability versus excessive leech tension. A stable upper leech holds its angle to the apparent wind as the boat pitches and the sheet load fluctuates. Stability comes from a leech loaded enough to resist flutter and opening, and the tension needed for it rises with wind speed. Beyond that point additional tension closes the leech, raises the effective angle at the head, hooks the top batten to windward and stalls the upper sail. The distinction is read at the top batten and top leech ribbon: a stable leech shows the ribbon streaming most of the time with the batten near parallel to the boom; excess tension shows the ribbon behind a hooked batten and a boat heeling and slowing together.

3.4.4Top-of-sail depowering without losing lower-sail drive. When the wind exceeds what the righting moment can hold, the upper sections are the cheapest place to shed load, because they heel the boat most per unit of drive. Opening the upper leech through twist reduces the head's angle of attack while the lower sail, whose drive is cheap in heeling moment, keeps its and angle. The Ovington manual notes that "Luff tension induces mast bend which will reduce power to the rig" S12, and Flynn states that more mast bend flattens and depowers the mainsail S33; both act along the whole span, so neither replaces twist, and in the order of section 2.3 the flattening step comes first. Diagonal wrinkles from clew to luff show over-bending S33 and mark the limit of the flattening route.

3.4.5Using vang and sheet independently to control twist and boom position. The mainsheet pulls the boom down and in together, so upwind it sets leech tension and boom angle at once. , and so sets leech tension independently of boom angle; downwind the vang controls leech twist when the mainsheet no longer pulls down S34. Upwind the vang sets the twist and the sheet sets the boom position for the mode, so the boom can move without the leech changing. The Ovington manual's warning that "it is easy to over vang the mainsail which will induce more mast bend than " S12 sets the limit: vang beyond it flattens instead of twisting.

3.4.6Dynamic twist changes in gust response and wave impact. A gust raises the apparent-wind speed and rotates it aft, so the effective angle at the head rises before the crew moves; the flat-boat response is an immediate ease that opens the upper leech, then a course adjustment and a re-sheet as the boat accelerates and the apparent wind returns forward. A wave impact removes speed and rotates the apparent wind aft while the rig unloads, so the sail is briefly at too high an angle with too little flow; the response is a small ease to re-attach flow and a steer that holds the entry angle through the pitch. In both cases the sheet moves first, as the fastest twist control, and the vang is reset afterwards.

3.4.7Gold-medal standard: retain stable upper leech and immediate post-disturbance acceleration. Viewed from a coach boat or on video, the top batten and upper leech ribbon hold a constant relationship to the boom through gusts, lulls and wave sets, with the ribbon streaming for the large majority of the time and no visible flutter or hooking. After each gust or wave impact the shows the boat returning to its pre-disturbance speed within a number of seconds the crew has set as crew value, with no second dip from a . Heel stays within the flat-is-flat band of plus or minus 5 degrees S36, and the sheet hand's movements are small and continuous rather than large and periodic.

3.6Gennaker flow and projection

The 38 m² gennaker S11 is the largest surface on the boat and is flown in the strongest apparent wind the boat makes, because the crew is "looking to really over the gennaker from quite early on in the wind range" S29. It works as a lifting sail with over most of its span rather than as a drag sail. Its efficiency depends on , on a stable luff, and on the coupling between sheet and course that the two details below describe.

3.6.2Luff stability, projection, and driving-force generation. Projection is the gennaker area presented to the apparent wind with the shape held open and stable. A projected gennaker flies its luff clear of the jib and mainsail, carries a leech open enough for clean exit, and holds its shape by rather than by sheet tension alone. Drive is lift at an apparent-wind angle that Ingham describes as basically remaining at 90 degrees S27S28, far enough forward that the flow crosses the sail rather than pushing it. A luff that collapses and refills breaks the flow each time, so luff stability is the first requirement; a luff that never moves is oversheeted, with an angle of attack too high for attached flow.

3.6.3Relationship between sheet tension, luff curl, and steering angle. Sheet, luff curl and course form one control loop. Easing the sheet reduces the angle of attack and lets the luff curl; steering lower increases the angle and re-attaches it. The target is a slight, continuous curl at the entry, held by a coordinated ease and small bear-away as the boat accelerates and the apparent wind moves forward. The Ovington manual names the failure mode: "Do not under trim the spinnaker as this will allow the leech to rotate up" S12, which unloads the sail and destroys projection. In extreme conditions the loop is inverted and the gennaker oversheeted to slow the boat, against the risk of pitchpoling when the boat ploughs into the back of a wave S29.

The sails supply the force, the hull and foils supply the resistance and the side force that reacts the sails' , and the crew supplies the righting moment. The four are one system: a change in sail force changes heel, heel changes leeway and foil load, and foil load changes the drag the sails must overcome. This section sets out the coupling and the decisions it forces.

3.7.1Sail-force vector and its effect on heel, leeway, and foil load. The sail force is the sum of and drag along it S19. Resolved into the boat's axes, a small forward component drives and a large lateral component heels and pushes sideways. The daggerboard and rudder generate side force only when the boat moves somewhat sideways S19, so the lateral component is reacted through leeway; most racing boats sail upwind in medium breeze with 3 to 5 degrees of leeway S23. Foil load rises with the lateral component and with foil load. : part of the lateral force becomes a downward push on the hull, the effective angle of attack falls, and drive per unit of sail force drops.

3.7.2Matching sail power to available righting moment. Righting moment is crew weight applied over the S25, and the 49er mast "can support a combined crew weight of up to 165kg from its dual trapeze" S9, while the available righting moment is fixed by crew and wing width once both are on the wire. Sail power is the variable. Below full power the crew adds angle of attack, depth and reduced twist; above it the crew removes power in the order of section 2.3 until heel returns to . Low trapezing and keeps the sail plan upright S25, so body position is treated as part of the righting-moment budget, not as a correction applied after trim.

3.7.3Using aerodynamic depowering to preserve hydrodynamic efficiency. Excess heel costs more than the drive it wastes. A heeled hull presents an asymmetric shape to the water, the daggerboard's effective vertical span shortens and its induced drag rises, and the increased leeway pushes the foils' angle of attack toward their own stall. Depowering the rig before heel builds keeps the foils working at low leeway and the hull on its designed lines, where and the forward chine turns water down for dryness and lift S14. The rule is that the sails are depowered to protect the hull and foils, and the cost of doing so is measured in speed and height together, not in sail area given up.

3.7.4Preventing excessive rudder angle caused by sail-plan imbalance. Rudder angle is drag with no driving return, and it arises when the sail-force and foil-force centres are misaligned so that the boat turns unless held. Raking the mast aft "creates weather helm", and the North Sails target is 3 to 5 degrees of weather helm in moderate conditions S32; the crew's own rake per band is crew value. Beyond that the sail plan is out of balance, usually because the mainsail is overpowered relative to the jib or the boat is heeled. Vang is released "considerably prior to bearing away" to S12, which is the same principle applied to a manoeuvre: unload the leech before the rudder has to fight it.

3.7.6Gold-medal standard: optimise sails, hull, foils, and crew as one system. An observer watching the boat through a wind band sees heel held within the flat band, leeway consistent with the foils working at low angle, rudder angle small and steady, and the crew's body height on the wire changing before, not after, the trim changes. When the wind rises the depowering sequence follows a stated order, flattening, then twist, then angle, and the speed and heading traces show no period in which the boat is both heeled and slow. The crew can state, for any observed adjustment, which hydrodynamic quantity it was made to protect, and the logged data confirm that height and speed moved together rather than one at the expense of the other.

3.8Aerodynamic performance process

Aerodynamic theory is only useful if it can be tested, and testing requires a reference, a comparison, a record and a decision. This section defines the process that turns trim experiments into settled knowledge. It relies on the loggers and recording equipment the class rules permit, on video, and on the crew's own observations, and it treats every conclusion as provisional until it has been reproduced. The crew's own logged values are the baseline throughout, and the process exists to make those values trustworthy.

3.8.1Establishing reference trims before each training block. A block begins by fixing the reference trim for each wind band: rig tension, rake, jib lead, sheet marks, vang mark, cunningham and outhaul positions, recorded as crew value. Rig tension is checked against the Ovington manual's Loos gauge references, forestay 40, side stays 28, intermediates 26 and backstays 17 with the mast stepped and no sails S12, which anchor the gauge reading while the crew's own tensions by band remain the racing reference. The reference is sailed first in every session so that the day's conditions are characterised against a known state. Any change to be tested is defined as a single departure from the reference, so that its effect can be attributed.

3.8.2Comparing modes against benchmark boats or performance baselines. Two-boat testing is the primary comparison: a benchmark boat holds the reference trim while the test boat sails the variant, in the same water and for long enough that the wind's cycles average out, and the roles are then swapped. Without a benchmark boat the comparison is against the crew's own baseline, built from log files as Njord builds polar targets and gain-and-loss comparisons S31. Published 49er polars by wind band were not found, so the crew's own targets by band are the reference, recorded as crew value. GPS data from an Olympic-class regatta show mean velocity and VMG increasing with wind velocity on both legs S30, so every baseline is indexed by wind speed before it is compared.

3.8.3Using video, speed traces, headings, and crew observations together. No single record explains a gain. Speed and heading traces from a logger such as the Vakaros Atlas 2, which logs at 2, 5 or 10 and reports VMG and shift tracking S79, or the Sailmon MAX, which records boat speed, heading, heel and pitch S80, show what happened; video from the coach boat shows the sail shapes and body positions at the time; the crew's observations supply the reason the change was made and what it felt like through the sheet and the tiller. The analysis aligns the three on , and a finding is accepted only when trace, picture and account agree. permits camera and sound recording equipment on board S3.

3.8.4Distinguishing repeatable gains from one-off environmental effects. A speed difference in a single comparison may come from the variant, from a difference in wind between the two boats, from a wave set, or from the wind's own cycle, in which 30 to 40 percent extra speed S50. Repeatability is established by reversing the boats' positions and roles, by running the comparison across at least one full oscillation of the wind, and by requiring the gain to appear on both sides of the swap. A gain that appears only on one side of the course, only on one tack or only in one wind band is recorded as conditional and is tested again under those conditions before it is admitted to the reference.

3.8.5Converting findings into concise on-water cues. A finding becomes useful when it is reduced to a cue that can be spoken in a few words and acted on without deliberation. The cue names the trigger the crew will see, the control that responds and the size of the response in the crew's units: for example a stated change in top-leech ribbon behaviour that calls for a stated vang step. Cues are written in the same language as the trim calls of 3.3.6, tested in training for a full block, and dropped if the crew cannot reproduce the response under race load. The cue list for each band is kept short, because a long list is not executed under pressure.

3.8.6Gold-medal standard: every adjustment has a trigger, purpose, outcome, and evaluation method. An observer reviewing a session with the crew can take any trim change from the video or the trace and hear the crew state four things without hesitation: what they saw that triggered it, what aerodynamic or hydrodynamic quantity it was meant to change, what the trace and the sail showed afterwards, and how they decided whether it worked. Changes that fail the fourth test are logged as untested and are not repeated in racing until they pass. The trim sheet for each band carries a revision history, and every entry cites the session and the comparison that justified it.
