The Racing Rules of Sailing 2025-2028 took effect on 1 January 2025 and, apart from urgent changes, are expected to stand until 2029 S1. This chapter treats them as a performance tool rather than as the vocabulary of the protest room. Section 13.1 sets out the right-of-way structure of Part 2 and how a crew converts it into early, low-cost decisions at skiff closing speeds. Section 13.2 covers the windward-leeward relationship that governs most same-tack interactions, and 13.3 the proper-course and luffing limits of rules 16 and 17 that bound it. Section 13.4 applies rule 18 to the zone, offset marks and gates, and 13.5 treats overlap as something the crew creates and breaks on purpose. Section 13.6 separates permitted boat handling from prohibited propulsion under rule 42, its 49er modification and Appendix P. Section 13.7 closes with protest-risk management: the decision, before and after an incident, between hailing, turning and moving on. The thread through all seven sections is that a right is worth only what it costs to enforce.

13.1Rights and obligations at high closing speeds

Part 2 rests on four right-of-way rules S1. Rule 10 states that "When boats are on opposite tacks, a port-tack boat shall keep clear of a starboard-tack boat", rule 11 that "a windward boat shall keep clear of a leeward boat" when overlapped on the same tack, rule 12 that "a boat clear astern shall keep clear of a boat clear ahead", and rule 13 that "After a boat passes head to wind, she shall keep clear of other boats until she is on a close-hauled course" W1. The section is about applying them before proximity forces the issue.

13.1.1Rule knowledge as a performance tool, not protest-only subject. A crew that knows its rights can sail closer to other boats without slowing, and that proximity is where lanes, laylines and mark positions are won. The mechanism is : when both sailors know which rule applies before the situation forms, the call is made once, early, and the boat holds its mode; when the rule has to be reconstructed under load, the helm hedges, the sheet eases and the boat loses speed. Rule 14 puts a floor under every right: "If reasonably possible, a boat shall (a) avoid contact with another boat" W1. Rights therefore define how late the crew may leave a decision, not whether it may skip it.

13.1.2Anticipating interactions before rights are contested. Two 49ers converging on opposite tacks close at the vector difference of their velocities, about 1.4 times boat speed on a beat and the sum of their speeds only when their courses are close to reciprocal, so the interval between sighting a crossing and acting is short relative to the time a decision takes. The crew classifies every converging boat at first sight into one of three outcomes, cross, duck or tack, together with the rule that will govern the meeting. A cross is committed only when the other boat's bearing is drawing aft; a steady bearing is a collision course and is resolved several lengths out, not at the crossing. The same classification applies downwind, where an apparent-wind angle near 90 degrees on an asymmetric S27 means convergences appear quickly from behind the gennaker.

13.1.3Communicating intentions early in converging situations. An early hail resolves the interaction cheaply because it removes the other crew's uncertainty about what the right-of-way boat will do. In a port-starboard convergence the starboard boat that intends to hold her course says so early and holds it; the port boat that intends to duck announces it so that no last-second luff or bear-away is provoked. Rule 16.1 makes the reason explicit: "When a right-of-way boat changes course, she shall give the other boat room to keep clear" W1. Inside the boat, the crew calls the other boat and its bearing trend while the helm names the intended outcome; the call is repeated only when the outcome changes.

13.1.4Safe margins without unnecessary loss of position. A margin is the distance at which the outcome no longer depends on the other boat's reaction. , sea state and how well the other boat is being sailed; the crew sets the crossing margin as crew value lengths for upwind crossings and crew value for downwind, and logs each case where a crossing was abandoned unnecessarily. The mechanism of loss is that a duck taken late costs a large bear-away and a re-trim, while a duck committed early is sailed as a small, fast course change that rejoins the lane with little distance lost. The test is whether the boat's speed through the crossing stays within its normal logged range.

13.1.5When tactical advantage does not justify collision or penalty risk. Rule 44.1 sets the price of a Part 2 breach: "A boat may take a Two-Turns Penalty when she may have broken one or more rules of Part 2 in an incident while racing" W1, and at Paris 2024 and the 2025 World Championship this was replaced by a one-turn penalty for the 49er S4S5. The decision rule compares the gain of the move with the certain cost of a turn plus the tail cost of damage or a hearing. A marginal cross gaining a length has a poor ratio against a turn losing many; a cross that decides which side of a persistent shift the boat sails may justify a duck-and-tack instead.

13.1.6Gold-medal standard: resolve interactions early, clearly, and at minimum cost. An observer following the boat sees every convergence resolved before the boats are within a few lengths, sees the right-of-way boat hold a straight course through the meeting, and hears one hail per interaction rather than a negotiation. The keep-clear boat's course change is small enough that its trapeze height and sheet settings barely alter. Over a regatta the log shows no contact, no turns taken for Part 2 incidents, and crossing decisions that were correct in the sense that the boat neither ducked a boat it could have crossed nor forced a boat it could not.

13.2Windward-leeward interactions

Rule 11 governs the most frequent same-tack relationship on a 49er course: "When boats are on the same tack and overlapped, a windward boat shall keep clear of a leeward boat" W1. Whether the boats are overlapped is settled by the definition, under which "They overlap when neither is clear astern" and "they also overlap when a boat between them overlaps both" W1. The section covers how an overlap is established from each side, what it obliges and what it grants, and the standard for entering and leaving the relationship.

13.2.1Establishing overlap and tactical implications. An overlap begins when the boat astern's "hull and equipment in normal position" cross the line abeam from the aftermost point of the other boat's hull and equipment W1; downwind that includes an extended bowsprit. At that instant rule 12 stops and rule 11 starts. Arriving to leeward converts the obligation to keep clear into a right, subject to the rule 17 proper-course limit when the overlap forms within two hull lengths W1; arriving to windward creates an obligation with no compensating right and leaves the boat in . The boat astern therefore chooses the side deliberately: leeward to hold a lane or gain rights at a mark, windward only to roll through with superior speed.

13.2.6Gold-medal standard: enter with a tactical objective and leave once achieved. An observer sees the boat form a windward-leeward overlap only when a named objective exists: a lane to protect, an inside position to secure before the zone, a boat to force onto the unfavoured tack. Once the objective is met, the boat separates by speed, angle or tack within a few lengths rather than remaining in a relationship that ties its course to another boat's. In the log the crew can state, for each overlap held beyond a brief interval, what it was for and what it produced; an overlap without an answer is counted as a defect.

13.3Proper course and luffing limits

The definition governs everything in this section: a proper course is "A course a boat would choose in order to sail the course as quickly as possible in the absence of the other boats referred to in the rule using the term. A boat has no proper course before her starting signal" W1. Rule 17 restricts a leeward boat that established her overlap from clear astern within two hull lengths W1, and rule 16 restricts any right-of-way boat that changes course W1. These two rules set the boundaries of every luffing contest.

13.3.1Tactical course changes versus improper luffing. Rule 17 reads: "If a boat clear astern becomes overlapped within two of her hull lengths to leeward of a boat on the same tack, she shall not sail above her proper course while they remain on the same tack and overlapped within that distance, unless in doing so she promptly sails astern of the other boat" W1. A leeward boat whose overlap began from clear ahead, or from beyond two lengths, is not so limited and may luff, subject to rule 16.1's requirement to "give the other boat room to keep clear" W1. The crew notes, as each overlap forms, how it formed and whether the luff available is unlimited or capped at proper course.

13.3.2Proper-course relevance by leg and sail configuration. Upwind the room between a close-hauled 49er's proper course and head to wind is its whole close-hauled angle, but speed falls away as soon as the boat goes above close-hauled, so a luff is self-limiting and any luffing contest is short. Downwind under gennaker the proper course is the VMG-optimal angle, which moves with pressure because the apparent wind on an asymmetric sits near 90 degrees S27 and moves forward as boat speed rises S26; a leeward boat bound by rule 17 may therefore legitimately head up in a lull because her proper course did, and a windward boat must anticipate that. Before the starting signal no proper course exists W1, so a leeward boat may luff to head to wind, limited only by rule 16.

13.3.3Defending position without sacrificing own performance. The leeward boat's defensive tool is small and early: a modest heading change that signals intent before the windward boat has built the speed to roll through, with the sails trimmed to the new course so that VMG is kept. The mechanism is that a windward boat rolling over a leeward boat needs several lengths of clear air to pass; if the leeward boat holds its lane high enough that the windward boat sits in , no further luff is needed. The crew's rule is that a defence which drops the boat below its target speed for more than a few seconds has already cost more than the windward boat would have taken.

13.3.4Avoiding luffing contests that destroy speed, control, or strategy. A full luffing contest in a twin-trapeze skiff puts both crews inboard, both boats near head to wind and both sail plans flogging, which ends with the pair behind every boat that sailed straight. It also fixes the boat's position on the course, forfeiting shift and pressure strategy. The rule is that a luff is used to make the windward boat choose between falling behind and a penalty, never to stop her; the leeward boat luffs to the angle at which the windward boat must respond, then returns to course as soon as the response is given.

13.3.5Communicating clearly during course-changing engagement. Rule 16.1 requires room to keep clear whenever the right-of-way boat alters course W1, so an announced luff is both courtesy and evidence that room was given. The leeward helm hails before altering and alters at a rate the windward boat can follow, while the crew calls the windward boat's response. Rule 16.2 adds: "on a beat to windward when a port-tack boat is keeping clear by sailing to pass to leeward of a starboard-tack boat, the starboard-tack boat shall not bear away if as a result the port-tack boat must change course immediately to continue keeping clear" W1. The crew treats it as a ban on late bear-aways at a ducking boat.

13.3.6Gold-medal standard: use rules-based pressure selectively for material outcomes. An observer sees the boat apply a luff or a proper-course hold only where the outcome changes something that survives the leg: a lane held to the layline, a boat forced across a favoured shift, an inside position secured before the zone. Luffs are short, announced and returned from at once; the boat's through the engagement shows a dip no longer than the alteration itself. Where the fleet sees a luffing pair, this boat is not in it; where it is, the log records the material result the luff produced.

13.4Mark-room and zone management

The zone is "The area around a mark within a distance of three hull lengths of the boat nearer to it. A boat is in the zone when any part of her hull is in the zone" W1; for a 4.99 m hull S11, a little under 15 m. Rule 18 applies between boats required to leave a mark on the same side when at least one is in the zone, but not between opposite-tack boats on a beat to windward W1. Mark-room is decided by the overlap at the instant the first boat reaches the zone S1.

13.4.1Identifying zone entry and overlap before congestion develops. Rule 18.2(a) fixes the relationship when the first of two boats reaches the zone: if overlapped, the outside boat gives the inside boat mark-room; if not overlapped, the boat that has not reached the zone gives it, and the obligation continues "even if later an overlap is broken or a new overlap begins" W1. Because the moment of entry decides everything, the crew fixes it: one sailor calls the zone edge as a distance, the other calls overlap state as the bow reaches it. Reasonable doubt runs against the boat claiming the change: "it shall be presumed that she did not" S1. A marginal overlap at the zone edge is therefore worthless.

13.4.2Planning approach to offset marks and leeward gates. At the windward mark and offset the boat plans the layline so that it arrives on starboard with the hoist unobstructed; an offset leg reached clear astern of a boat rounding ahead gives inside rights nowhere and a hoist in bad air. At a gate, the mark farther upwind is closer, the least crowded mark is preferred and the down-current mark turns faster S58; a pinwheel at the right-hand gate mark can pin a boat indefinitely S59. The decision rule is to choose the gate mark by the next beat's plan and by the rounding crowd, then to enter the zone with the overlap state already known and called.

13.4.3Inside and outside positions at high-speed roundings. The classic rounding swings wide on the near side, cuts close on the far side and has the boat close-hauled before the bow reaches the mark S51; an inside position approaching the mark is worth fighting for S51. At skiff speed the inside boat's mark-room is room to sail to the mark and round it, not room for a tactical rounding, so an inside boat that enters too fast or too wide loses the exit it was entitled to. The outside boat's mechanism is to slow early, let the inside boat go, and take the wide-in, tight-out line behind, which frequently places her to windward and ahead on the next leg.

13.4.4Mark-room rights, clean exit, and next-leg position. Mark-room is a means to the next leg, not an end. The boat holding it plans the drop, the vang release, which at a windward mark is made "considerably prior to bearing away" S12, and the turn so that the exit lane is clear of the boat that gave room and of boats arriving on the layline. At a leeward gate the exit tack is fixed before entry and the rounding is shaped to reach it at full speed, close-hauled and on the trapeze. Where the exit lane will be dirty, the crew accepts a slower rounding for a cleaner lane, because a lane is worth more than a length gained at the mark.

13.4.5Avoiding late claims that create contact risk or poor geometry. A late claim is an overlap called after the zone or a hail for room that the other boat cannot satisfy. Rule 18.2(d) removes protection from a boat that "obtained an inside overlap from clear astern or by tacking to windward of the other boat" where the outside boat "has been unable to give mark-room" from the time it began W1, and rule 14 binds both boats throughout W1. The crew's rule is that any overlap it cannot call with at the zone edge is treated as absent: the boat drops astern, rounds outside and keeps its speed, rather than entering a contest that the presumption rule S1 is already set to decide against it.

13.4.6Gold-medal standard: plan rights, handling, exit speed, and next-leg lane together. An observer hears the crew name, before the zone, the rights it will have, the manoeuvre it will make, the speed it will carry out and the tack it will leave on, and sees all four happen as named. Mark-room claims are made only from positions the crew confirmed before entry; roundings are wide-in and tight-out; exits are on the planned tack with no boat directly upwind. The log shows crew value as the target for speed retained through the rounding, and the season record shows no hearing arising from a mark incident.

13.5Overlap creation and overlap breakage

Overlap is a state the crew manufactures, holds or dissolves rather than one it discovers. The definition fixes it geometrically: a boat is clear astern when her hull and equipment in normal position are behind a line abeam from the aftermost point of the other boat's hull and equipment W1, and the terms apply to boats on opposite tacks only when rule 18 applies or both are sailing more than ninety degrees from the true wind W1. Because overlap decides rule 11 against rule 12, the reach of rule 17 and mark-room, its timing is a controllable performance variable.

13.5.1Creating overlap before the zone when tactically valuable. An inside overlap established well before the zone converts into mark-room at the moment the first boat reaches it S1. The mechanism is speed differential applied at the right angle: a boat with slightly higher VMG builds the overlap from clear astern by sailing the line that puts its bow abeam of the other boat's stern from the inside, then holds that line with trim rather than helm. The value test is whether the overlap will still exist at the zone with margin to spare; an overlap that will be marginal at the zone edge is not pursued, because reasonable doubt is presumed against it S1.

13.5.2Breaking overlap to regain manoeuvring freedom. An overlap binds both boats: the windward boat to keep clear, the leeward boat to her proper course under rule 17 if she came from astern W1, and each to the other's course. Breaking it restores the freedom to tack, gybe or change mode. The mechanism is to open fore-and-aft distance across the line abeam: the boat astern eases and drops back, the boat ahead accelerates or heads up to draw her stern clear. Outside the zone a broken overlap changes the rule 18 outcome only if the boats then reach the zone clear astern and clear ahead; the crew therefore breaks overlap early enough that the new state is settled before the zone edge.

13.5.3Using speed and angle to control overlap timing. Overlap timing is set by relative speed and by the angle of the line abeam. Sailing slightly lower and faster lets a boat astern arrive on the leeward side of that line at a chosen moment; sailing higher and slower delays it. Because the line is abeam of the leading boat, that boat also moves the line by changing heading, and a small luff swings it forward across the pursuing boat's bow. The crew treats the last few lengths before a mark as a timing problem: the target is to cross the zone edge with the overlap state the plan needs, and speed is modulated to hit it.

13.5.4Recognising strategically harmful overlaps. Some overlaps cost more than they give. A leeward overlap gained from astern inside two hull lengths caps the boat at its proper course W1 and pins it beneath a windward boat that controls the lane; a windward overlap places the boat in with an obligation and no right; an overlap on the wrong side at a gate forces the rounding at the crowded mark. The rule is that an overlap is judged by its exit: if it cannot be broken when the crew wants to tack on a header or gybe on a lift, it is harmful and is avoided before it forms.

13.5.5Communicating overlap status precisely within the crew. The sailor with the better view calls the state in a fixed vocabulary that distinguishes overlapped, clear astern and clear ahead, and adds the side, so that the helm hears both the rule in force and the room available. A change of state is called at the moment it happens, with the distance to the zone if a mark is near, because the moment fixes rights. Doubtful states are called as doubtful, never rounded up to a claim. The crew's own protocol defines the words and the ; the standard is that no overlap call is repeated because it was unclear.

13.5.6Gold-medal standard: use overlap deliberately, never as a last-second ambiguity. An observer sees every overlap the boat holds near a mark established or broken with margin, called inside the boat as it happens, and hailed to the other boat only when the crew has certainty. At the zone edge the state is known by both sailors and by the other boat. The log contains no incident in which the crew's claim rested on an overlap that formed or broke within a length of the zone, and no hearing in which the boat's overlap evidence was the deciding question.

13.6Rule 42 and kinetic technique

Rule 42 permits only wind and water as sources of propulsion S1. The 49er class rules relax it in one respect, allowing sculling "if the sculling does not propel the boat in any direction" S3. Enforcement at major events runs under Appendix P, where an observer who sees a breach penalises the boat "by, as soon as reasonably possible, making a sound signal, pointing a yellow flag at her and hailing her sail number" S2. This section separates fast legal technique from marginal technique and sets the crew's response to a penalty.

13.6.1Permitted boat handling versus prohibited propulsion. The distinction is between body and sail movements that trim the boat to the wind and water and movements that push the boat through them. Trapezing, moving weight to set heel and fore-and-aft trim, a single sheet pull to initiate surfing or planing where the rule's exceptions allow, and steering to follow waves are handling; repeated rocking, repeated pumping, sculling that drives the boat and ooching are propulsion. The 49er relaxation permits sculling only where it does not propel the boat in any direction S3, so the test is the direction of the resultant force on the rudder, not the size of the tiller movement.

13.6.2Managing body movement, roll, and sail handling under observation. Twin-trapeze sailing produces large, legitimate body movements in every manoeuvre, and an observer classifies them by pattern: one movement per wave or per gust reads as trim, a rhythm reads as propulsion. The crew therefore ties each movement to a visible external cause and lets the boat settle before the next. Heel is held flat, within the plus or minus 5 degrees that skiff coaching guidance calls acceptable S36. Sail handling is single and decisive: one sheet adjustment per change in apparent wind, not a series of small pumps. World Sailing's guide notes that 49er breaches "do occur in the 4-8 knot wind range" S18, so discipline is tightest there.

13.6.3Maintaining performance without marginal or inconsistent technique. Marginal technique is technique the observer must decide about; inconsistent technique is technique that looks different at the end of a race from the start. Both invite a flag. The performance rule is that any movement whose gain the crew cannot demonstrate in the log is removed, because a small gain minus the cost of a penalty is negative. Consistency is trained by fixing the per wind band and rehearsing it under , so that what the observer sees in the last race matches the first. The crew records its own kinetic pattern per band as crew value descriptions for review against video.

13.6.4Preparing for umpired and closely observed environments. At Paris 2024 and at the 2025 World Championship the two-turns penalty and the Appendix P penalties were replaced by a one-turn penalty for the 49er, and the event documents state which penalty applies S4S5. Preparation therefore starts with reading the notice of race and sailing instructions for the penalty in force, the arrangements for observer boats, and any restriction on communication such as the Paris 2024 ban on voice, radio or data transmissions while racing S4. On the water the crew sails as if observed at all times, so that observed and unobserved technique are the same technique and nothing changes when an observer boat appears.

13.6.5Responding correctly to an on-water penalty situation. The signal is a sound, a yellow flag pointed at the boat and a hail of the sail number S2. The correct response is immediate and unargued: the helm acknowledges, the crew calls the penalty in force from the event documents, and the boat takes it as prescribed, getting "well clear of other boats as soon after the incident as possible" and "promptly making the required number of turns in the same direction, each turn including one tack and one gybe" W1. The decision rule is that the turn is taken where it costs least, but at once; delay invites a further penalty and argument gains nothing.

13.6.6Gold-medal standard: develop fast, legal technique robust under fatigue and pressure. An observer with a yellow flag watching the boat for a full race sees no movement it needs to think about: body and sail movements each match a visible wave, gust or manoeuvre, occur once, and are followed by a settled boat. The pattern in the final race of a series is the pattern of the first. The speed log shows the boat holding its targets in the light band where breaches concentrate S18 without kinetics that would draw a flag. The season record shows no yellow flag, and any penalty received was completed within seconds of the hail.

13.7Protest-risk management

A protesting boat "shall hail 'Protest'" at the first reasonable opportunity under rule 60.2, and the default time limit for lodging one is two hours after the last boat finishes under rule 60.3 S1, cut to 60 minutes at the 2025 World Championship S5. Starting penalties escalate under rule 30 from the I flag round-an-end requirement to the Z flag 20 percent scoring penalty, the U flag disqualification unless the race is restarted, and the black flag disqualification that survives a restart S1. This section treats exposure to all of these as a cost to be managed.

13.7.2Tactical gain versus probability and cost of protest. Every rules engagement carries three prices: the turn the boat may have to take, the hearing it may have to sit through and the disqualification it may receive. The decision rule weighs the tactical gain against the probability of a protest and the cost of losing it, and that cost is asymmetric: a place gained is one point, while a disqualification is scored behind the whole fleet, and the discard that might have absorbed it is limited to the worst score S1. The same asymmetry applies at the start, where a black-flag disqualification survives a general recall S1, so an aggressive line position under that flag is priced against the whole race, not a length at the gun.

13.7.4Keeping post-race accounts factual and consistent. An account that will hold in a hearing is one recorded at once and in the same words by both sailors: the tack, the overlap state, the distance to the zone, the hail made and heard, and the course changes in order. The crew writes it down as soon as the race ends, before discussing it, and compares the two versions; disagreement between them is resolved by the log data rather than by persuasion. The account never includes what the crew inferred about the other boat's intention, only what it saw and heard. Consistency, not eloquence, is what a protest committee weighs.

13.7.5Internal process: protest, take penalty, or move on. The process runs in three questions asked in order, within seconds of an incident. First, whether the boat may have broken a rule; if so, it takes the penalty in force at once, because the turn is cheaper than a hearing and rule 44 exists for a boat that "may have broken" a rule W1. Second, whether the other boat broke a rule and the boat wishes to protest; if so, the hail "Protest" is made at the first reasonable opportunity S1 and the account is recorded. Third, if neither, the incident is closed and not discussed until ashore; the time limit is checked from the sailing instructions, which set 60 minutes at the 2025 Worlds S5.

13.7.6Gold-medal standard: prevent avoidable exposure rather than win disputes later. An observer sees the boat sail a series with no hearing it initiated from a marginal position and none it received from a marginal move; where a penalty was due it was taken at once, and where a protest was justified it was hailed at once and filed within the limit. Starts under the U and black flags are made without a recall against the boat's number. The regatta log lists each incident with its three-question outcome, the time the account was written and whether the two accounts matched; the standard is that they always do.
