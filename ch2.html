The 49er carries one hull, one set of spars and one mainsail, jib and gennaker through an entire event under the class rules S3, so the whole wind range has to be covered by settings rather than by inventory. This chapter treats the rig as a single elastic structure and the sails as shapes that the structure imposes on cloth whose design is fixed. Section 2.1 establishes how mast bend, rake, prebend and set the mainsail's depth and the boat's helm balance. Section 2.2 works through the stays and spreaders that produce that bend and the forestay tension that governs the jib. Sections 2.3 and 2.4 move from structure to shape: mainsail depth, twist and leech behaviour, then jib entry and between the two sails. Section 2.5 covers the running controls that move shape inside the static envelope from gust to lull, and section 2.6 assembles the whole into mode-specific settings across the wind range, with the crew's own logged values as the reference wherever no published 49er figure exists.

2.1Mast bend, rake, prebend, and rig stiffness

A mainsail is cut with , and its flying depth is the difference between that curve and the bend of the spar it is hoisted on. North Sails therefore frames rig tuning as four goals: eliminate side bend and lean, set rake for helm balance, set pre-bend to match the mainsail design, and control mast bend and headstay sag S32. The first keeps the two tacks identical, the second sets the steering load, and the third and fourth decide how full the mainsail is at rest and how far it can be flattened under load.

The mechanism is geometric. Bending the mast ", which simultaneously flattens the sail, opens the leech and moves the draft aft" W3, while "A straighter mast creates shape and power" W4. In the 49er the bend while sailing comes from the sail controls: " which will reduce power to the rig" S12, the vang bends the lower mast through boom compression, and the mainsheet loads the topmast through the leech. Each control bends a different part of the spar, so the mix sets the depth distribution , not merely the average depth.

Prebend is the bend present with the mast stepped and no sail hoisted, produced by spreader sweep and stay tension. North's guidance is "somewhere between an inch and a few inches of pre-bend" S32, matched to the mainsail design S32, so that added load moves the mast forward in the middle and flattens the sail W4. Too little prebend leaves the sail deeper than its design; too much removes depth the light-air mode needs and cannot be recovered on the water. sits above prebend: cap shroud tension stays at the baseline to about 11 to 12 knots and goes " for progressively stronger winds" S13, so stiffness caps the bend the running controls can find.

Rake sets helm balance. "creates weather helm" S32 because "Tilting the whole sail plan aft shifts the power aft" W4. North's general target is 3 to 5 degrees of weather helm in moderate conditions S32; the 49er target is crew value. The Ovington manual places the "approximately 2.7m above the sheer line" and "350-450mm behind the mast" in the windward set-up W2; rake moves that lever, and "adding rake is a tool for generating power in light air; reducing rake is part of the de-powering process" W4. No published 49er rake figures by wind band were found; the crew's logged rake per band crew value is the reference.

On the water the crew reads three signals: side bend by sighting up the mast on each tack W5, helm with the boat flat, because "" W4, and over-bend from the diagonal creases running from the clew towards the luff S33. A fault on both tacks is a rake, prebend or stiffness fault and is corrected between races; a fault on one tack only is side bend and is corrected in the stays and spreaders.

2.2Shroud load, spreader geometry, and forestay tension

The stays form one . The cap shrouds carry the mainsail's heeling load to the chainplates and, because they run aft of the mast, also hold the forestay straight; the lowers and intermediates support the middle of the spar; the spreaders convert shroud tension into sideways and forward push at their height. The Ovington manual gives Loos gauge references with the mast stepped and no sails: forestay 40, side stays 28, intermediates 26, backstays 17 S12, and describes its figure 4.1 as the initial set-up "for all crews in winds of 8 to 12 kts" W2. Those numbers are a calibration point, not a race setting: cap tension stays at that set-up to about 11 to 12 knots, "stronger for heavier crews" W2, and goes progressively tighter above S13. The crew's base tensions per band are crew value.

Spreader geometry decides how the tension is spent. The lower spreaders sit "around the base of the topmast with the band against the top of the lower mast", the upper spreaders "around the reinforcing at the hounds", and the windlass cords of both pairs are set "level and level with each other" W2. Sweep gives the compressive load at the spreader root a , which is the source of prebend; length sets the sideways lever that stops the middle of the mast falling off to leeward. A longer or more swept spreader therefore gives more prebend and more for the same cap tension, and unequal cords give a mast that bends differently on each tack. North's check is to sight up the mast on each tack and, "If the middle sags to leeward, tighten the lowers" W5.

Forestay tension is the jib's shape control, and in a swept rig it is a by-product of cap tension. When the headstay sags "the headsail becomes fuller and more powerful, which is great in light conditions", and sag is reduced as the breeze builds W4. Sag also rounds the entry and moves the draft forward, which widens the steering groove in light air and chop at the cost of pointing. Bend and sag interact: excessive mast bend softens the whole rig and increases headstay sag W4, because it brings the hounds closer to the deck so that "the headstay goes soft" W4. Jib luff tension is a separate control , "almost slack in light airs, grading to very tight in really strong winds" W1, with enough tension "to remove scallops between the hanks" W2.

The crew uses these relationships as a diagnosis tree. A jib too round in the entry while the mainsail is already flat means the cap tension is too low for the wind, not that the jib is sheeted wrongly. A mainsail that flattens more on one tack than the other points to spreader or stay asymmetry rather than to trim. Because a single set of spars is allowed per event S3, any change to spreader cords or stay lengths alters the only rig available, so it is made ashore, measured with the gauge, and logged before it is raced.

2.3Mainsail depth, twist, leech stability, and depowering

Gladstone names : angle of attack, depth and twist S20. "More depth creates more power and better acceleration; while a flatter sail has less power and less drag", and "Increasing twist reduces power; decreasing twist adds power" S20. On the 49er's 16.1 m² mainsail S10 depth is set by mast bend against and by the outhaul, and , with Dellenbaugh's general target about 50 percent of the way from luff to leech W3. The battens are fitted tight, carry no adjuster that can be worked while sailing, and stay in the sail W1, so depth is a rig and control problem only.

Twist exists because , which creates "a stronger apparent wind and a wider apparent wind angle aloft" S21. Gilbert's model-rig figures, about 4 degrees close-hauled and about 12 degrees with the wind abeam S22, are not 49er values, but the direction transfers: the twist required rises as the apparent wind angle opens. Dellenbaugh's rule is to trim the mainsheet "until the top batten is parallel to the boom" S34, with the top batten telltale streaming aft "between 50 and 90% of the time" W3; if the top of the sail lifts before the bottom it is too twisted, and if the bottom lifts first it needs more twist W3. The twist setting also decides how the top of the sail behaves through a spectrum in which wind speed rises and falls by about 7 percent every 6 to 12 seconds S50.

Leech stability is the rig's ability to hold a constant exit angle while the load fluctuates. A leech that pumps open and shut varies driving force and heeling moment together, so neither helm nor crew can settle. The Ovington manual identifies the main cause: over-vanging "will induce more mast bend than luff curve, resulting in inconsistent pressure in the rig when sailing" S12. A leech is stable when the bend matches the luff curve, the draft sits where the sail was designed to carry it, and the top batten returns to the same angle after every gust.

Depowering follows an order. "Power can be reduced either by increasing twist or flattening the sail (or both)" W6, and because a flat sail carries less drag S20 the first step is flattening: mast bend through cunningham and vang, and a firm outhaul. "A well-behaved main should 'blade out' when the mast reaches maximum bend" W3, and the over-bend creases S33 mark the end of that step. The second step is twist, opening the upper leech with the mainsheet W6; the third is static, cap tension set tighter between races S13. The trigger is righting moment: the mast is rated for "a combined crew weight of up to 165kg" on the trapezes S9, and once both sailors are fully out and the boat still heels, the sequence starts. A correctly depowered mainsail shows the top batten parallel to the boom or slightly open, the telltale streaming, no creases from the clew, and a boat that stays flat through a gust without a luff.

2.4Jib entry, slot control, and jib-main interaction

The 49er jib is 5.1 m² S10, sheeted on a curved self-tacking track with limit pins and a clewboard with several sheet holes S13. Three variables set its shape: forestay sag and luff tension fix the entry, the clewboard hole fixes the ratio of leech to foot tension the sheet applies, and the pin position fixes the sheeting angle and hence . Its value lies less in its own force than in what it does to the flow over the mainsail.

Entry is the angle the luff presents to the apparent wind. A sagging forestay makes the jib fuller S33 and rounder in the entry, which widens the steering groove and suits light air and waves; a straight forestay and a hard luff give a finer entry that points higher but demands more precise steering. Luff tension is graded from "almost slack in light airs" to "very tight in really strong winds" W1, since "As wind pressure increases so must the luff tension" W2; more luff tension pulls the draft forward and stops the entry becoming a knuckle as the leech loads up.

The slot is controlled with the pins, the clewboard and the sheet. The class guide sets the pins innermost "for winds up to about 12kts and flat water, a little further out for stronger winds and/or rougher water" S13 W1, and the sheet in the middle hole to about 12 knots with "progressively lower holes for stronger winds" S13 W1. The Ovington table runs the clewboard from the top hole at 0 to 8 knots, through the middle hole at 12 to 17 knots, to the bottom hole from 18 knots, with the pin moving from hole 1 to hole 5 across the same bands W2. Moving the sheet attachment down the clewboard sends more of the load along the foot and less down the leech, so the top of the jib twists open; moving the pins out widens the slot and the sheeting angle. Both depower and both widen the helm's groove in rough water.

The interaction between the sails is why the slot matters. bends the flow ahead of the jib so that the jib carries more load than it would alone; the jib's downwash reduces the angle at the mainsail's luff, so the main can be sheeted tighter before its front section stalls. Dellenbaugh's balance point is a main whose "forward section 'lifts' evenly from boom to headboard, at the same time as the genoa's windward telltales lift" W3. "Lots of backwind in the mainsail means that the slot is too narrow" W3, and a jib leech that is too round acts as a narrower slot, because curvature in the aft part of the leech "kicks air into the mainsail" W3. The jib is therefore trimmed to the main's mode: a bow-down speed mode takes the pins out and the sheet eased; a height mode brings them in and firms the sheet, until the main starts to backwind. Under gennaker the jib is "well eased so only bottom half of jib is drawing" W2.

2.5Cunningham, vang, outhaul, and dynamic shape control

The static rig defines an envelope of shapes and the running controls move inside it. The wind the boat meets is not the mean: Bethwaite's measurements show speed rising and falling by about 7 percent every 6 to 12 seconds and gusts carrying 30 to 40 percent extra speed S50. Cap tension and rake cannot follow that; only the cunningham, vang, outhaul and mainsheet can keep the mainsail matched to the wind actually on the sail.

S34, and in the 49er it is also the primary bend control: "Luff tension induces mast bend which will reduce power to the rig" S12. They are linked: the more the mast is bent, the tighter the cunningham must be pulled to keep the draft in place W3, since a bent mast with a slack luff carries its draft aft, the opposite of depowering. In light air the cunningham stays loose W3. Downwind it is released "to prevent overloading the tack area of the sail as the mast straightens (thus lengthens) under spinnaker" W2, which also returns the depth the run needs W3.

The vang is calibrated ashore. The procedure is to pull the mainsheet on "until 'overbend' creases just start to appear on the main diagonally from the clew to the spreaders", then take the vang to that tension and mark the rope as a maximum reference W2. The mark exists because over-vanging induces "more mast bend than ", which produces "inconsistent pressure in the rig" S12. Upwind and leech tension, so the mainsheet can be eased in a gust without the sail powering up. Downwind the vang controls leech twist because the mainsheet no longer pulls down on the boom S34; it is released "considerably prior to bearing away" to S12, then retensioned on the new course "to similar tension as upwind" W2.

S34. The manual's practice is a "firm outhaul in most conditions", eased "Only when under powered with crew off the trapeze" W2, upwind and downwind alike. It also acts on the lower leech: easing "adds depth to the foot, which in turn closes the lower leech", and tightening opens it W3. The outhaul is eased slightly when the waves are big for the wind and pulled on when they are small for the wind W3. On the 49er it is a mode setting changed at the ends of the range, not a gust control.

Dynamic control is a hierarchy by speed of response. The mainsheet is fastest and manages twist through each gust and lull; the cunningham manages bend and draft as the mean rises within a leg; the vang and outhaul are set for the leg; the rig is set for the race. A control is never used for a problem one level below it: a gust is answered with sheet, a rising mean with cunningham, a wrong base shape with vang or outhaul at the next mark, and a rig wrong for the day with cap tension between races.

2.6Mode-specific tuning across the wind range

The class rules force a tuning matrix. One set of spars and one mainsail, jib and gennaker are allowed per event unless lost or damaged beyond repair, and North 3Di mainsails, North jibs and MacDiarmid gennakers are prescribed at the Olympics and in gold-fleet racing S3. The bands are defined by righting moment rather than wind speed alone: the rig is rated for "a combined crew weight of up to 165kg" S9, and they run from under-powered with a sailor off the wire, through fully powered with both on the wire and the boat flat, to over-powered where flat is held only by depowering.

The under-powered band targets maximum power and the widest groove. Rake is aft, since "adding rake is a tool for generating power in light air" W4; cap tension is at the set-up baseline S13; the jib sheet is in the top clewboard hole and the pin in hole 1 for 0 to 8 knots W2, the luff "almost slack" W1, the sheet eased about 25 mm W2, the cunningham loose W3, and the outhaul eased only while the crew is off the trapeze W2. The top batten is parallel to the boom S34 or slightly open W3. Logged values are crew value.

In the fully powered band the rig is at its design point. The jib sheet is in the middle hole and the pins innermost to about 12 knots in flat water S13, moving to holes 2 and 3 between 12 and 17 knots W2. Jib sheet is firm W2, the vang is at or below its mark W2, and cunningham comes on as the mean rises. Wave state decides the mode: pins "a little further out" for rougher water W1, outhaul eased slightly when the waves are big for the wind W3. Base values are crew value.

In the over-powered band the order of section 2.3 governs. Cap tension goes "" S13, later for heavier crews W2; the jib sheet moves to lower holes S13, the bottom hole from 18 knots with the pins in holes 4 and 5 W2; the jib luff is very tight W1; the cunningham is hard and the mast bent to the over-bend creases S33; reducing rake is "part of the de-powering process" W4, and with no published 49er rake figures by wind band the rake step is crew value. The test is both sailors fully extended, the boat within the "Flat is FLAT" tolerance S36, and the mainsail blading out in gusts without the leech pumping.

Downwind mode is the same across the bands, except at the top of the range, where the gennaker is over-sheeted to slow the boat against the pitchpole risk S29. before the bear-away and retensioned on course S12 W2; the cunningham is released W2; the jib is eased until only the bottom half draws W2; the 38 m² gennaker S11 is trimmed with the boat heeled to windward "with the windward chine in the water" and never under-trimmed S12. Each crew value entry is tested in a two-boat pairing before it becomes the base.
