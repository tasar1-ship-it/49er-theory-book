4Hydrodynamics and Foil Control

The hull and the two foils of the 49er convert sail force into forward motion, and the crew's control of them decides how much of that force becomes speed and how much is lost as drag. This chapter treats the four underwater variables the crew adjusts continuously: longitudinal trim, which sets wetted area and which part of the hull carries the boat; heel, which sets how the foils and the sail-force vector are presented to the water; daggerboard depth, which sets the balance between sideforce, drag and control; and rudder angle, which records every imbalance the other three leave behind. The sections build on each other. Trim and heel determine the flow the daggerboard sees, the daggerboard determines how much leeway and heel the rudder has to correct, and the rudder angle is the final measure of whether the whole system is balanced. The 49er planes but does not foil, so the theory concerns the displacement, transition and planing regimes of a light skiff hull and nothing else.

4.2Wetted area and longitudinal hull trim

Longitudinal trim decides which part of the hull the water touches. Near , is about a third of the total, wave resistance another third, and form and induced drag about 10 per cent each S19. Buoyancy carries a hull below a Froude number of about 0.4 and hydrodynamic lift above 1.0 to 1.2 S24; on the 4.99 m hull S11, taking overall length as waterline length, those thresholds are about 5.4 and 13.6 knots, so the class's 9 to 10 knots upwind W3 lie in the transition. The fastest trim therefore changes with speed.

4.2.2 Bow immersion, , and excessive rocker engagement. Bow immersion buries the and chine, sections meant to turn water down for lift S14, which push it aside, and in waves it invites the pitchpole of ploughing into a wave at speed S29. Stern drag comes from an immersed transom: Laser tank tests measured over 30 per cent extra resistance from transom-immersed trim at 4.5 knots, yet the same trim cut resistance by around 3 per cent when planing at 8 knots and above W2. Excessive rocker engagement is a fast boat trimmed bow-down: the curved forward bottom meets the flow at a negative angle and sucks the hull down rather than lifting it. The rule is bow-down in displacement, level in transition, stern-down only when planing.

4.2.3Fore-and-aft crew position by wind strength and sea state. In light wind the hull is in displacement, friction dominates and the crew moves forward to shorten the wetted length and lift the transom clear; Laser tank data show bow-down trim cutting drag area by 2.2 to 3.6 per cent at 2 knots, a gain residuary resistance cancels from 3 knots upward W2, a mechanism, not a figure, that transfers to the 49er. Through the transition the crew moves aft so . When planing the weight goes further aft, but not so far that the transom drags or the bow slams. Chop shifts the schedule aft, because the bow needs more reserve buoyancy. Base positions per wind band are crew value.

4.2.4Dynamic movement through waves and acceleration phases. Trim is a movement. Approaching a wave face upwind the weight goes aft so the forward V meets the wave rather than punching it, and over the crest it comes forward to drive the bow down the back and keep the hull attached. On acceleration the weight moves aft, because the fastest trim moves aft with speed W2, and it comes forward in the lull before the sinking feeling as the hull comes off the plane W3 immerses the stern. Rule 42 bounds this: only wind and water may propel the boat S1, so movements that trim the hull are legal while rocking and pumping are not, and 49er breaches occur most in the 4 to 8 knot range S18.

4.2.6Gold-medal standard: treat longitudinal trim as a continuous adjustment, not a static setting. The observable standard is that the crew's fore-and-aft position is never still for more than a few seconds while speed or sea state is changing, and that the transom leaves a clean wake with no turbulent water behind it. Video from astern shows the bow wave forming at the same station on the hull in every wind band, the immersed length shortening as the boat speeds up and lengthening as it slows. On a logged track, boat speed and the crew's position read from onboard video move together, with position leading speed on acceleration rather than following it. A trim reset only at mark roundings fails this standard; one adjusted with every gust, lull and wave passes it.

4.3Heel, leeway, and sideforce management

Heel and leeway are the angles through which the daggerboard works. The daggerboard and rudder generate side force only when the boat moves somewhat sideways S19, and most racing boats sail upwind in medium breeze with 3 to 5 degrees of leeway S23. Upright, the angle of attack over the daggerboard equals the leeway angle W1, so leeway is not a fault but the price of sideforce. Heel tilts the foil, tilts the sail-force vector, and makes the underwater body more asymmetric W1. The crew holds the heel that gives the foils the cleanest angle of attack.

4.3.2 Cost of excessive heel: leeway, , and unstable loading. Heeled to leeward, the daggerboard's span tilts, its tip swings to windward so that part of its force acts upward instead of sideways, and the foils are less effective when not vertical W5. Leeway rises, the sail-force vector tips so that part of its side force becomes a bow-burying downward push while drive falls because heel lowers the angle at which the sails meet the flow, and the adds its own . The helm holds rudder to windward, adding rudder W5; in Day's Laser velocity prediction, rudder angle accounted for around 20 per cent of hydrodynamic drag upwind W1. Loading is also unstable, because heel shortens the crew's lever and removes righting moment when the gust demands it. Skiff coaching states the tolerance as flat, plus or minus 5 degrees acceptable, plus or minus 10 only just tolerable S36.

4.3.3Cost of excessive flatness: reduced feel, turning authority, or wave management. With no heel the helm goes neutral and the tiller stops reporting balance; the signal comes from the 3 to 5 degrees of weather helm of the moderate-air target S32. Turning authority falls: heel gives an asymmetric shape that turns the hull without rudder, and roll rotates the boat through a tack with little rudder drag, the reason the chine is removed amidships for roll tacking without a large drag increase W3. Heel is not always a loss: Day found at 9 knots of wind that heel's residuary penalty was smaller than its frictional gain from reduced wetted area W1. In a steep sea an over-flat boat lands its full chine on each crest; a small heel presents the V.

4.3.4Matching heel angle to upwind, reaching, and downwind mode. Upwind the target is flat within the skiff tolerance S36: the foils are vertical, leeway is minimal and crew weight works as righting moment. Reaching under gennaker the apparent wind is forward and the sideforce large, so the flat target holds, with a sharp puff absorbed by a brief heel before weight and sheet catch up. Downwind the sign changes: the boat sails heeled to windward with the windward chine in the water, and the gennaker is not undertrimmed, since too little sheet load lets the leech rotate up S12. Windward heel lifts the leeward chine clear and tilts the sail-force vector so that part of it lifts and its lateral part shrinks, which reduces the leeway the daggerboard must make. Heel targets by mode are crew value.

4.3.5Using crew weight and rig adjustment to manage sideforce efficiently. Sideforce is met by the daggerboard, but the heeling moment it produces is met by crew weight and the rig. Righting moment is crew weight applied over the longest available lever S25, and low trapezing and keeps the sail plan upright S25. Weight is the first response because it costs no power; when both sailors are fully extended and the boat still heels, the rig is depowered until the sideforce falls to what the weight can hold. which reduces power S12 and the mainsheet then opens the upper leech; if the boat is still over-powered, cap shroud tension goes between races S13, in the order of section 2.3. Depowering before the lever is exhausted gives away drive.

4.3.6Gold-medal standard: hold heel target through gusts and wave impacts with minimal helm correction. An observer sees a mast that stays within the plus or minus 5 degree tolerance S36 through a gust, with the correction coming from the crew's bodies and the sheets and not from the tiller. The wake behind the rudder stays straight and the tiller extension moves in small angles. On logged data, heel from the boat's sensor holds a narrow band about its target while wind speed varies, and the , where recorded, shows no spikes at gust onsets. A recovered with a luff is a failure of this standard; one recovered with a hike and an ease is a pass.

4.4Centreboard loading and rake

The 49er's daggerboard is a fixed-rake foil in a case, and the class rules make hull appendages manufacture controlled and permit one daggerboard per event S3. The crew's control over it is therefore depth: how much of the span is below the hull. Depth sets the area and available to make sideforce, the induced drag that the sideforce costs, and the position of the relative to the sail plan. Rake is set by the case, and its effects are understood so that balance can be tuned around it rather than with it.

4.4.1Centreboard depth as a balance of sideforce, drag, and control. A foil makes sideforce in proportion to area, angle of attack and , and pays in induced drag, about 10 per cent of resistance near S19; a short, deep foil raises the ratio of lift to energy dissipated by vortices W4. Full depth gives most sideforce for least leeway, the upwind requirement. As speed rises the same sideforce needs less area, because lift grows with speed squared while the sails' demand does not, so surplus board becomes wetted area and drag. Raising it cuts area and aspect ratio together: less drag, less grip. For control, a deep board resists skid and gives the rudder something to turn against; too little lets the stern slide.

4.4.2 Rake effects on balance, helm feel, and . Rake moves the daggerboard's centre of lift fore and aft. Raking the board aft carries its lift toward the rudder and reduces weather helm; raking it forward adds weather helm, as does S32. In the 49er board rake is fixed by the case and is a constant the crew records once, so helm balance is tuned with mast rake, sail trim and heel. Directional stability follows from the separation between daggerboard and rudder: a longer lever makes the boat hold its heading, a shorter one makes it more agile and nervous, and that lever is part of the boat's character, not an adjustment. The measured board rake and mast-rake base settings are crew value.

4.4.3Board settings for height, speed, and survival modes. Height mode upwind uses full depth: maximum area and aspect ratio, minimum leeway, at a drag cost. Speed mode, where the helm foots for pace, carries the same depth because the extra speed reduces leeway; the board is raised only when the boat is planing and sideforce comes from a small angle of attack at high speed, on the gennaker legs. Survival mode downwind in strong wind raises it further, to reduce the tripping moment when the boat slides sideways and to make a capsize less likely to become a pitchpole; oversheeting the gennaker to slow the boat S29 belongs with it. Board heights by mode and wind band are crew value; no published 49er table was found.

4.4.4Board-position changes during transitions and tactical phases. Board moves happen where the sideforce demand changes, never while the foil is heavily loaded. The board is raised after the bear-away, once the boat has accelerated, and lowered before the drop and the rounding onto the wind, so it is at full depth when sideforce returns. considerably before bearing away to reduce leech and rudder loads S12 follows the same logic: unload first, then change geometry. In the gybe a deeper board gives the boat a pivot and a shallower one lets it slide; on a reach the sideforce is large and the board stays down. Before a crowded rounding the board goes down early, since a late move competes with sheet and tiller for hands.

4.4.5Interaction between board loading, heel angle, and sail-force vector. When the boat heels to leeward the board tilts, its effective span shortens and its tip swings to windward, so part of its force acts upward instead of sideways, while the sail force tilts so that part of its side force presses down and drive falls because heel lowers the angle at which the sails meet the flow; leeway rises. Windward heel does the opposite: the board's tip moves to leeward and part of its force acts downward, the sail's side force tilts upward and its lateral part shrinks, and the asymmetric hull adds a to leeward, bearing the boat away, for the rudder to balance. That geometry is why the board can be raised downwind with the windward chine in the water S12. Upwind, low trapezing that keeps the sail plan upright S25 keeps the board vertical and its lift horizontal, the most efficient loading.

4.4.6Gold-medal standard: pre-plan board changes by mode without compromising coordination. Every board move happens at a pre-agreed point in the manoeuvre sequence, called by one sailor and executed by the other. On the water the board goes down before the drop and the leeward mark, up after the set once the boat is at speed, and to the survival height before the top mark in the strong-wind band, without the sheet or the tiller being released to do it. On video the change occupies one hand movement and does not coincide with a or a course change. The plan by mode and wind band is written down with crew value entries; a board left at the wrong height for a whole leg is a failure of the plan.

4.5Rudder load and steering efficiency

The rudder is a small foil doing two jobs: it steers the boat, and it makes up whatever the rest of the system leaves unbalanced. Both jobs cost drag, and the second can be reduced to nearly nothing. In Day's dinghy velocity prediction, rudder angle comprised around 20 per cent of total hydrodynamic drag upwind W1. Every degree of tiller held against an imbalance in heel, trim, sail balance or board depth is drag the boat could have removed; the aim is a boat that steers itself and a helm who intervenes only to change course.

4.5.1Rudder angle as an indicator of system imbalance. A held rudder angle is a measurement, not a fault. Weather helm means the sail plan's acts behind the lateral resistance, or the boat is heeled; the tiller is pulled to windward and the rudder adds W5. Lee helm is the reverse and slower, because the rudder's lift opposes the daggerboard's. A weather helm of 3 to 5 degrees S32 gives the helm pressure to read and some useful rudder lift; anything beyond it names a cause: excess heel, mainsheet too hard for the jib, mast rake too far aft, or the board too deep for the downwind mode. The cause is removed with weight, sheet or board, not held on the tiller.

4.5.2 Deliberate steering input versus drag. Deliberate steering changes the course: a wave, a gust, a mark. It is efficient when heel, sheets and weight move with the turn, so the rudder only starts a rotation the hull and sails complete. Corrective steering holds the boat against something else and is pure drag: the rudder makes a force that is cancelled by the force it corrects. The tiller shows the difference: deliberate inputs are large, brief and return to centre; corrective inputs are small, constant and never return. Foils generate lift more effectively when moving through the water slightly S35. A rudder that is never quite still and never held far off centre is efficient. A persistent imbalance is corrected at its source.

4.5.6Gold-medal standard: use small, anticipatory helm inputs and a boat state requiring little correction. An observer sees the tiller extension moving in small arcs, ahead of each wave and gust rather than after it, and lying near the centreline between inputs. The wake behind the rudder is straight, with no turbulence from a blade. Recorded rudder angle, where the crew logs it, shows a distribution centred near the weather-helm target S32 with a narrow spread and no sustained offset on either tack, and , because heel is corrected by weight and sheet rather than by the tiller. A boat that tracks straight for several seconds in flat water when the helm releases the tiller is the direct test of this boat state.
