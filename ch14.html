Equipment optimisation in a manufacture-controlled class is the discipline of bringing the same boat to the start line in a known, recorded state every day, with nothing on it that can fail during the race and nothing on it that can fail measurement. This chapter treats the boat as a measured system. Section 14.1 sets out the rig-tension and geometry protocol and the logging that makes a setting reproducible. Sections 14.2 and 14.3 cover inspection of the spars and standing rigging and preparation of the daggerboard and rudder, the parts of the boat that the class limits to one per event. Section 14.5 treats and purchase as a trim-resolution problem, and 14.6 applies the same logic to the gennaker system, where a snag costs a leg. Sections 14.7 and 14.8 deal with the fittings that carry the highest loads, with what may lawfully be repaired, and with the spares and redundancy that stop a minor failure becoming a retirement. Section 14.9 closes with the class rules effective 1 October 2026 S3 as the boundary of the whole optimisation space.

14.1Repeatable measurement-controlled rig set-up

A rig setting exists only when it can be reproduced from a written record without reference to how the boat felt. The baseline every log starts from is one the crew sets itself: with the mast stepped and no sails hoisted, the gauge reading on each wire is recorded as crew value. The published tuning tables do not label the wires the way the class rules do, with their upper, middle and lower shrouds W1, so the log states which wire each reading refers to on this boat, and every later entry is a departure from the baseline expressed in gauge units and turnbuckle turns rather than adjectives. The class tuning page gives the direction of travel, cap shroud tension at the baseline up to about 11 to 12 knots and " for progressively stronger winds" S13, but no table by wind band; the crew's own logged values are the reference and appear here as crew value.

The mechanism linking tension to sail shape is direct. Cap shroud tension sets forestay tension and therefore headstay sag, and sag makes the jib fuller S33. Lower and intermediate tension set how far the middle of the mast may bend under mainsheet and vang, and more bend S33. Rake sets helm balance, since "creates weather helm", with a target of 3 to 5 degrees of weather helm in moderate conditions S32. North Sails states the four tuning goals as eliminating side bend and lean, setting rake for helm balance, setting pre-bend to match the mainsail design, and controlling mast bend and headstay sag S32. Each is measurable: a gauge reading, a rake measurement from a fixed reference on the hull, a pre-bend measurement against a taut halyard, and a sighting up the mast track on both tacks.

Repeatability comes from fixing the protocol, not from the gauge. The same gauge is used every time and checked against a second gauge at intervals; it is applied at the same height on the same wire, with the wire free of spreaders, tape and covers; and the reading is taken after the rig has been loaded and released once so that . The forestay length is itself a class quantity, measured as its extension beyond the mast heel when laid along the forward face of the mast, and must lie between 425 mm and 435 mm W1; the forestay is fitted to the centre hole of the stem head fitting W1. Because "Standing rigging shall not be adjusted after the start" W1, every setting is committed before the warning signal, and a pre-start change is decided against the logged wind band and the forecast, not against the last minute of feel.

The log entry that makes a setting reusable contains wind range, sea state, the reading for each wire, rake, pre-bend, spreader position, the jib sheet hole and track pin position (the class guide's middle hole and innermost pins up to about 12 knots in flat water is the published baseline S13), the batten set, and an outcome that can be checked: mast straight sideways on both tacks, overbend creases appearing from the clew toward the spreaders only at the intended mainsheet load W2, and helm load within the target band. On the water the test of a correct set-up is symmetry: identical trim, mast bend and helm load on both tacks. An asymmetry is a set-up defect or an inspection finding, never a trimming problem, and is recorded for the next day's rig rather than compensated with sheet.

14.2Mast, shroud, spreader, and forestay inspection

The rig is a single set for the whole event. Rule C.9.2 states that "Only one set of spars and standing rigging shall be used during an event except when an item has been lost or damaged beyond repair", and replacement requires the approval of the ICA Technical Committee W1. An inspection therefore protects the series, not only the day. The standing rigging is 1 x 7 stainless steel wire rope W1; the forestay, middle shrouds and lower shrouds are between 3.0 mm and 3.5 mm in diameter and the upper shrouds between 2.3 mm and 2.6 mm W1. Wires of that size carry the righting moment of a crew of up to 165 kg on the trapeze S9, and the failure mechanism is , where the wire bends under every cyclic load.

Inspection starts where the wire stops: the swages, the T-ball that fits into the key plate at the hounds W2, the shroud plates, and the turnbuckles the rules permit between the wire end and the hull fixing point on the lower shroud and between the shroud plate and the hull fixing point on the cap and primary shrouds W1. The signs are broken strands at the swage mouth, a kink, discolouration and a pin worn oval. Rig pins may be replaced by quick release pins or any other type W1, and each pin is checked for its retainer. The lower parts of the forestay and shrouds may be covered with protective material provided the function of the fittings is not changed W1; because covers hide the wire, they come off for the inspection.

Spreaders are set horizontally and level with each other, sighted with the boat on its side and the mast centreline horizontal W2. The rules allow them to be permanently bonded to the middle section provided the lower section remains removable, to be fixed to the shrouds by bonding, fastening or tape, and to be reinforced with additional composite laminates W1; at the Olympic Games and in gold-fleet racing only spreaders manufactured after March 2022, identified by local thickening at the inboard end, and only CST masts may be used W1. The inspection checks the seating of each tip on its shroud, cracks at the inboard root and movement of the band, because a spreader that has rotated or crept shows on the water as a mast that bends differently on each tack. The mast sections may be permanently glued together at the top spreader W1; the joint, the sail track, the halyard sheaves, the gooseneck, the strop carrying the vang take-off block W1 and the trapeze attachment, where additional safety lines may be added W1, are each loaded by hand and watched for play. Mast sections may be clear coated only with prior approval and prepared only with a soft non-abrasive pad W1, and the rig is rinsed with fresh water after sailing in salt W2.

On the water the rig reports its own condition. A Loos reading that drifts between days without adjustment or an unseated terminal; a change in helm balance at the same rake indicates that the forestay length or a spreader has moved; asymmetric mast bend indicates a spreader or lower shroud fault. Each is an inspection trigger logged with date, finding and action. No published replacement interval exists for these wires, so replacement follows findings, and a wire with a visible defect is replaced before the next race rather than watched.

14.3Centreboard and rudder preparation

The daggerboard and rudder generate side force only when the boat moves somewhat sideways S19, and most racing boats sail upwind in medium breeze with 3 to 5 degrees of leeway S23. A foil's task is to produce that side force at the least drag, which depends on its section and planform, fixed by manufacture control, and on surface finish, edge condition and the rigidity with which it is held. The class allows one daggerboard and one rudder blade per event S3, and only blades identifiable by the embossed 49er logo may be used W1. Preparation therefore means finish, edge repair, fit in the case and stock, and alignment.

The permitted work is narrow and sufficient. Cleaning and polishing the hull appendages is permitted W1, and "Repairs to chips in the leading and trailing edge may be filled and blended in" W1; anything beyond that is a localised repair that "shall not be used to reinforce an existing part or add a function" and needs written approval before it is attempted W1. The mechanism behind the edge rule is : a nick in the leading edge disturbs the flow over the behind it, and a thick or ragged trailing edge sheds a wider wake and can hum. The board's exposed area "has a dramatic effect on performance both up and down wind" W2; area is controlled by raising and lowering, and the maximum up is 200 mm, beyond which the board fouls the crew and equipment when tacking W2. The case packing may be replaced with any material but shall not extend more than 60 mm into the trunk from the top or bottom W1. Packing sets the play of the board: slop lets it rotate under load so that its effective angle changes from tack to tack, and a case that is too tight cannot be raised while loaded in the bear-away.

The rudder is held by the stock, the pintles and the gantry. The rules permit the rudder stock packing to be replaced with any material, the tiller forward of the stock to be modified and the tiller extensions to be replaced without restriction W1; a spare tiller extension is mandatory W1. Play anywhere in that chain creates a in which the tiller moves and the blade does not, so the helm's input arrives late and is then overcorrected. The blade is checked for vertical alignment and for its angle to the centreline, and the weather helm target of 3 to 5 degrees S32 is achieved through mast rake, not through rudder rake or play. Foils "are much more effective at generating lift" when moving through the water S35, and the lift they make depends on the angle at which they are held, so the rudder must be holdable at a steady, small angle without the noise of slop.

On the water the checks are helm feel, hum and the bear-away. A humming trailing edge, a board that sticks when raised, or a lag between tiller and bow is a preparation fault. Before an event the crew fits board and blade, raises and lowers each under load, checks the retention that stops the board floating out in a capsize, polishes both foils, records any edge repair, and marks the board's raised positions at crew value so that area is set by mark rather than by estimate.

14.5Control-line friction and purchase efficiency

The quantity being optimised is trim resolution: how small an adjustment each sailor can make from the wire, in both directions, at speed. and friction taxes it, and friction costs more on the ease than on the pull, because a control that will not run out cannot be released in a gust. The Ovington manual warns that "it is easy to " and that " which will reduce power to the rig" S12; both controls therefore need fine, reversible adjustment rather than raw force. The class limits set the frame: the cunningham purchase may be increased to a maximum of 6:1 and the rope purchase of the vang system to a maximum of 4:1 W1, and a bail may be fitted beneath the jib blocks with a single block added to increase jib sheet purchase W1.

Friction accumulates at every sheave, at every bend of a line around a sheave too small for it, at every fairlead the line rubs, and at the cleat entry angle. The rules allow any block to be replaced with a block of the same number of sheaves of similar or greater diameter, and ratchet blocks have no sheave diameter restriction and may be used for the gennaker sheets and as the forward mainsheet block on the boom W1. Sheets and lines may be replaced without any restriction as to length, diameter and taper providing the part is not made of wire W1, which permits tapered sheets that keep grip at the hand and run thin through the blocks. A continuous main sheet and jib sheet is permitted W1, wedges or risers of any material may be fitted under the wing-mounted cleats W1 to correct the lead angle, and a jib downhaul may be led aft using a single thimble but no sheaves or cleats W1. Ball bearings are kept clean and free from salt, and the boat and its fittings are rinsed with fresh water after sailing W2.

The boundary is that "Running rigging shall be led through and attached to the fittings supplied for their function" W1 and that the boom vang take-off block shall be attached to a mast strop W1. Optimisation therefore lives in line choice, sheave size, lead angle, cleat position within the permitted adjustments, take-up arrangements with blocks, thimbles, clips, rings or balls on shock cord W1, and maintenance, not in re-routing controls.

The test is done at the dock and repeated under sailing load. Each control, uncleated, must run out under its own load; the cunningham must release with the vang on; the vang must be adjustable one-handed from the trapeze by either sailor; and the gennaker sheet must run cleanly through its ratchet when the crew eases. Friction grows with salt and wear, so the ease test is repeated after every block of sailing days, and the line diameters, purchases and block choices that pass it are recorded as crew value so that a replacement reproduces the same resolution.

14.6Gennaker-system reliability

A gennaker of 38 m² S11 is set and dropped by a chain of parts, halyard, sock, pole launch, tack line, sheets and retrieval, and the reliability of the chain is the product of its links. The sailing plan assumes an immediate, clean set and drop: considerably before the bear-away to reduce leech and rudder loads S12, the sail is not under-trimmed because that lets the leech rotate up S12, and in extreme conditions it is oversheeted to slow the boat S29. None of that is available if the hoist stalls or the drop jams, so the equipment is prepared to deliver the manoeuvre, not merely to survive it.

The halyard is the link that fails most quietly. It chafes where it exits the mast, at the sheave and at the cleat, and it elongates until the head stops short of full hoist. The rules permit the halyards to be replaced by lines of any material and to be led externally W1, and they allow a take-up system of shock cord, rings and rope blocks with a sheave diameter of not more than 20 mm W1 plus a block behind the two floor blocks with a sheave of not more than 30 mm W1, so that the tail is gathered without adding friction to the hoist. The manual's method for feeding a new internal halyard is to run water down inside the mast at the fastest rate possible and feed the halyard in from the top W2. The bowsprit is identified by white safety banding at each end and at the forward end cap fitting W1, and the pole is pulled out through a sheave on the deck W1; the launch line and its return are checked for chafe at that sheave, and the sock mouth is checked for tears that catch the foot on the drop.

The sheets are retained by fittings of flexible material along the rail forward of the chain plates W1, which stops the lazy sheet dropping under the bow on a gybe or a drop. At the Olympic Games and in gold-fleet racing the gennaker is the MacDiarmid sail identified by two horizontal seams W1, and the retrieval patches and their line are inspected as part of the sail. Routine sewing, mending and patching is permitted, sails shall not be recut, and graphics may be cut in only outside the head, tack, outer panels, luff, leech and foot as the rule defines W1; the manual's guidance is to inspect the sails periodically and have a sailmaker restitch or add doublers wherever wear starts to appear W2.

The reliability test is a full hoist and drop before every warning signal. The halyard reaches its full-hoist mark, set at crew value; the sheets run free through both ratchets; the tack line cleats and releases; the sail drops to the bottom of the sock without a stop. A hoist that stalls, a sail that will not drop, or a tack line that jams is fixed before the start, because a compromised system converts every leg decision into damage control. After any capsize the whole chain is re-tested before the next start.

14.7High-load fittings, fasteners, and failure prevention

at attachment points, and the highest on a 49er are at the chain plates and shroud plates, the stem head fitting, the mast step, the hounds and forestay key plate, the gooseneck and vang strop, the trapeze attachments on the mast and wings, the wing-to-hull joints, the rudder gantry and the jib track. The class allows fasteners to be replaced or added if the function of the fitting or part is not altered, allows fittings to be bedded in provided they can be removed without damage, requires replacement fittings to be fitted in the same position as the original, and restricts other fittings to the same model or a replacement model W1. No holes may be made in the hull or deck mouldings except for the listed fittings, for repairs and for the compass retaining lines W1, the existing holes in the jib sheet track may be enlarged to 6.5 mm but no additional holes are permitted W1, and "Any repair shall not be used to reinforce an existing part or add a function" W1. Because reinforcement is unavailable, prevention is inspection and timely replacement.

The failure mechanism is . Every tack and every gust reloads each fitting, and the righting moment from a crew of up to 165 kg on the trapeze S9 is carried into the hull through a few fasteners. A fastener that has loosened lets the fitting move, the hole elongates, and the steady load becomes an that cracks the laminate or shears the fastener. in salt water corrode at the interface, water entering a loose fitting degrades the core, and a crack that starts at a rivet hole propagates with each cycle. The rules require the watertight integrity of the hull to be maintained and the breather hole in the centre plinth to remain open and unrestricted W1; water inside the hull is therefore both a rule matter and the first symptom of a fitting failure.

The inspection routine is mechanical rather than visual alone. With the rig tensioned each fitting is loaded by hand and watched for movement; witness marks across nut and fitting show rotation since the last check; black streaks show fretting; weeping shows a bedding failure. The hull with its permanently fixed fittings shall not weigh less than 94.0 kg S3, and of not more than 3.0 kg may be fixed only at the positions the rule names W1, so weight is not an argument for lighter fittings. Trapeze wires may be replaced with stainless steel wire of not less than 2.0 mm or lines of not less than 3.0 mm, and additional safety lines may be added at the attachment point W1.

On the water the boat reports failures early as sound and movement: a creak on the tack, a shroud plate that shifts, a gooseneck that clicks. Each is logged and traced ashore the same day. The decision rule is that a fitting showing movement is already failing and is replaced, with approval where the rules require it, before the next racing day. Protective coverings of soft material over exposed fittings are permitted W1 and protect sails and bodies, but they hide defects, so the inspection is done with the covers off.

14.8Spares, redundancy, and on-water repairs

Redundancy is allocated by consequence: what removes the boat from the race, what can be fixed between races, and what waits for the shore. The class makes a spare tiller extension mandatory W1 and allows spare parts and tools, removable for weighing W1; the use of shock cord or adhesive tape is in general unrestricted, except that it may not create a new fitting or extend the function of a permitted one W1. The kit therefore sits in three places: a minimal set on the boat, a heavier set on the coach boat, and replacements at base. The decision rule for an on-water repair is that it is attempted when it restores the ability to sail the next race safely; otherwise the boat takes the coach-boat part or retires from that race.

What may be replaced without approval covers most failures. Any cleat may be replaced with one of any material and substantially the same size and design; any block with one of the same number of sheaves and similar or greater diameter; rig pins with quick release pins or any other type; sheets and lines without restriction as to length, diameter and taper; halyards with lines of any material; trapeze wires with stainless wire of not less than 2.0 mm or lines of not less than 3.0 mm W1. Battens may be secured with cable ties and their length altered to adjust pocket tension, but they shall not be ; routine sewing, mending and patching is permitted and chafing patches may be added to the mainsail W1. Localised repairs to damaged equipment require that the ICA Technical Committee, or at an event the event measurer, is advised and approval sought before any repair is attempted, in writing for the appendages and the rig W1, and the hull, spars, foils and each sail are limited to one per event except when lost or damaged beyond repair, with replacement only on approval S3.

The on-water kit is built from that list: tape, spare shackles, rings and pins, a spare sheet, a spare trapeze handle, cable ties, a sail-repair patch and a knife; its contents are crew value and are checked against the boat's actual fittings. The repairs that succeed on the water are lashings and substitutions that reproduce the original function: a lost rig pin replaced by a spare, a chafed sheet swapped end for end or replaced, a batten re-tied, a small gennaker tear patched dry. A repair that would change a function, reinforce a part or require drilling is not attempted on the water, because it would be a rule breach as well as a poor repair.

Preventive care is the cheapest redundancy. The boat and all fittings are rinsed thoroughly with fresh water after sailing in salt W2, the main luff tape and track are sprayed with silicon to reduce wear on the bolt rope around the batten ends W2, and the sails are inspected periodically with wear referred to a sailmaker W2. After any repair, on the water or ashore, the boat is re-checked for function and compliance before the next start, and the repair is logged with the approval reference where one was required.

14.9Measurement compliance and equipment risk

The class rules effective 1 October 2026 S3 define the space inside which every optimisation in this chapter is made. "49er hulls, hull appendages, rigs and sails are manufacture controlled" S3, and each of rules C.6 to C.10 opens with "The following is permitted without the approval of the ICA Technical Committee" W1; anything not on those lists is a question for the Technical Committee, not a crew decision. Equipment risk has two components, a compliance failure at measurement or on protest and a reliability failure on the course, and both remove the boat from the result.

The per-event limits make selection a series decision. One hull, one set of spars and standing rigging, one daggerboard, one rudder blade and one mainsail, jib and gennaker may be used per event unless lost or damaged beyond repair S3, and any replacement requires approval W1. , so the selection rule weighs speed in the expected wind band against durability and range, and the crew's rule is crew value. At the Olympic Games and the other games the rule lists, and to qualify for gold-fleet racing at the class World and European Championships and the named regattas, only the North 3Di mainsail, the North jib (or a jib modified by North Sails and registered with the ICA and/or North Sails) and the MacDiarmid gennaker identified by two horizontal seams may be used, and sails with the North serial numbers listed in Appendix A shall not be used S3 W1. The same events restrict the boat to CST masts and to spreaders manufactured after March 2022 W1; and at every event, not only those, the bowsprit must carry the white safety banding and the foils the embossed logo W1.

Measurement checks a short list of physical facts. The hull, with wings, sock, bowsprit, rudder stock, gantry, permanently fixed fittings and foot straps, but without daggerboard, rudder blade, control lines and portable equipment, shall not weigh less than 94.0 kg S3, with of not more than 3.0 kg fixed at the named positions W1. The forestay extension beyond the mast heel lies between 425 mm and 435 mm W1, the forestay is fitted to the centre hole of the stem head fitting, standing rigging is not adjusted after the start, and the wings are fully extended when racing W1. Battens are not and sails are not recut W1. Portable equipment under C.5.2 is limited to timing devices, a maximum of two compasses without functions beyond heading, heading memory and timing, camera and sound recording equipment, and race tracking equipment "where permitted by the ICA, Notice of Race and/or Sailing Instructions" S3, all removable for weighing W1; the Paris 2024 Notice of Race additionally prohibited voice, radio or data transmissions while racing S4. A logger that displays anything beyond those functions is, at measurement, a prohibited electronic compass, so the crew reads the event documents for what a given device may do at that regatta.

Risk is managed by rehearsal and traceability. Before the event the boat is measured against the current rules by the crew, the serial numbers of sails, spars and foils are recorded, and every fitting on the boat is traceable to a permitting rule or a written approval; the crew's compliance checklist is crew value. The standard is that a measurer given the boat and its file finds nothing to ask about, and that no item on the boat is there because it was always there.
