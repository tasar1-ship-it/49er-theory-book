This chapter treats each 49er manoeuvre as a sequence of states: an entry speed, a turn, a crew crossing, a sheet handover and an exit acceleration, with a loss attached to each state and a place where that loss is paid. Section 8.1 sets the baseline with the tack, the manoeuvre the boat performs most often, and 8.3 places it in the gust and wave field where its cost changes. Section 8.4 works through the gybe as a swing of the apparent wind, and 8.5 isolates the gennaker, whose rotation and sheet load govern every downwind transition. Section 8.6 covers the top-mark transitions, the bear-away, the gybe set and the offset leg, where the vang and the pitchpole limit dominate; 8.7 covers the gate, where the drop and the wide-then-tight rounding decide the first tack of the next beat. Section 8.8 closes with loss of control, treated as physics and sequence. Throughout, body movement stays inside rule 42 S1, and the loss accounting rests on the crew's logged values rather than on published figures.

8.1Tack mechanics and exit-speed preservation

A tack costs the gap between the VMG the boat actually made and the VMG it would have made sailing straight, integrated from the jib ease to the moment both sailors are back on the wire at full speed. Turn duration is only one term; a fast turn that exits slowly costs more than a slower one that carries speed through head-to-wind. The Ovington manual states why the 49er pays more than a conventional dinghy: it enters faster, its rig "will develop more aerodynamic drag due to its size so the 49er will slow down at a greater rate", there is "a greater distance to cover during the tack", and there is "the added task of the skipper getting onto the trapeze" W1.

The entry state is a flat or windward-heeled boat at full speed: "Keep the boat flat or heeled to windward, especially prior to the tack" W1, because windward heel keeps the boat out of leeward heel while both sailors leave the wire. The jib is eased before the helm moves, "about 100mm in normal conditions, 200mm in fresh to frightening" W1. The turn is not a constant arc: the helm steers "in slowly to a maximum rate of turn about 70% of the way through" W1. A slow initial rate keeps the rudder angle small while the boat is fastest and highest; the foils, "much more effective at generating lift" when moving through the water slightly S35, carry the boat up on . The maximum rate comes near head-to-wind, where speed is lowest and the bow must be driven through rather than left to hang.

The crossing is the state with the largest righting-moment gap. In the skipper-first sequence the helm moves off the wire onto the wing with the mainsheet in hand, unhooks, and "calls the final stage of the tack and pushes the helm down" W1; in the crew-first sequence the crew eases and unhooks, the helm heads up as the crew moves in across the boat, and the helm pulls up "vertically on the trapeze wire" as the boat rolls to windward W1. Crew-first puts the trimmer on the wire early so the jib is sheeted from the wing; skipper-first puts the helm out early so the is steered from the wire. In both, nobody is in the cockpit while the mainsail fills on the new side.

The boat leaves head-to-wind a few degrees below close-hauled with both sails slightly eased so they fill at a wider apparent-wind angle; as it accelerates "the apparent wind speed increases and the apparent wind angle decreases, moving further forward" S26, and the sheets come on with it while the bow comes up. Trimming to the final angle at once stalls both sails. Rule 42 bounds every movement in the sequence: only wind and water may propel the boat S1, the class rules relax the rule only for sculling "if the sculling does not propel the boat in any direction" S3, and World Sailing's 49er guide notes that breaches "do occur in the 4-8 knot wind range" S18, where a roll-assisted tack is most tempting. The crew's tack cost per wind band is crew value.

8.3Wave- and gust-timed tacks

The cost of a tack is not fixed; it depends on the pressure and the water the boat turns in, so the decision after "tack" is "where". The wind rises and falls by about 7 percent every 6 to 12 seconds and 30 to 40 percent extra speed S50. A tack begun as a gust arrives exits into rising pressure and the acceleration is shortened; a tack begun at the start of a lull exits into falling pressure and the acceleration runs into nothing. The rule is to tack into the gust and never into the lull, and to enter with speed: the manual's advice for the bear-away, that cracking off a couple of degrees before the mark means "Even an extra two knots will make a big difference" W1, applies equally to the tack.

Waves change the geometry. In a wave's phase speed is about 1.56 times its period in metres per second S73, so the crests arrive at intervals regular enough to be timed. The bow passes through head-to-wind with the least speed and the least steering authority; if it coincides with the bow meeting a wave face, the wave stops the hull and the tack stalls. The turn is therefore started on the back of a wave so that the boat is head-to-wind in the trough and falls onto the new tack down the back of the next crest, with the wave pushing the bow off the wind rather than into it. Leftover waves running at an angle to a new breeze put one tack into the waves and the other across them, and each needs its own mode S77; the tack that exits onto the into-wave tack is the expensive one.

Crew timing follows the boat. The crossing coincides with the eye of the wind, where heeling force is least, and in waves the deck is also moving, so the crossing is made in the trough while the boat is level. The helm calls early enough for both sailors to pick the wave, and the jib ease is the trigger. In a gust the initial turn rate is slightly lower, because is higher at speed, and the exit heading is slightly lower too, because the stronger apparent wind moves forward faster once the boat accelerates.

The timing window is subordinate to the tactical call when the two conflict. A 5-degree shift changes the relative position of two boats by 12 percent of their lateral separation S52, and when that value exceeds the tack cost the boat tacks on the header and accepts the poorer wave; when the values are close, waiting one wave or one gust is cheap. The 49erFX study found extra manoeuvres on the first and last legs S37, and concluded that training should "aim to enhance manoeuvring efficiency, more prominently in strong wind conditions" S30: a well-placed tack is not a cost centre, a badly placed one is. The crew's tack cost by wind band and sea state is crew value.

8.4High-apparent-wind gybes

The 49er gybe is a swing of the apparent wind, not of the true wind. With the gennaker set the apparent-wind angle "basically remains at 90 degrees" S27. Apparent wind is the of true wind and boat course S19; as the boat squares up, more of its speed subtracts from the true wind and the apparent wind moves aft and weakens. On the new gybe the apparent wind moves forward again and its speed recovers as the boat heads up. The gybe therefore has a load minimum in the middle and two maxima at the ends, provided the boat keeps its speed. If it slows, the apparent wind while both sailors are inboard and least able to hold the boat, which is the mechanism of the gybe wipe-out.

The manual's timing rule follows: "Gybe just after having accelerated in a gust or running down the face of a wave, as at this point the apparent wind will be at its least but most importantly the boat will have the greatest speed and therefore the helm will have the greatest amount of control" W1. Maximum speed also minimises the boom load at the crossing. Two further rules apply: "Keep the 49er flat. Be positive." W1, and "The skipper must always look forward" W1, because the exit heading is chosen against the wave and gust ahead, not felt through the rudder.

The sequence has two variants. In the skipper-first gybe "The skipper moves in first, onto the wing or into the cockpit", calls once settled, the crew moves off the wire into the boat, and "The crew goes straight across the boat and sits on the new wing, well out" W1. In the crew-first gybe "The crew passes the spinnaker sheet to the skipper and moves into the boat" and takes "a bent-over position with the old sheet in one hand and the new sheet in the other" W1, while the helm calls, drops the mainsheet and pulls up onto the new side. The losses sit in three places: at the crossing, when neither sailor is on a wing and heel leaves the skiff coaching band in which "+/- 5 degrees is acceptable" S36; at the sheet handover, when the gennaker collapses (section 8.5); and at the exit, if the helm heads up before the crew is out and the sheet is on.

The exit state is below the target angle. The manual's two-sail instruction, "Steer the boat quite square out of the gybe until crew has control of balance" W1, holds with the gennaker: the helm heads up progressively as the crew moves out and the apparent wind builds. Heading up early loads the rig while the crew is inboard; heading up late leaves the boat slow, deep and exposed to the next gust. Without the gennaker the boat slows faster and the apparent wind rotates aft faster, so the two-sail gybe carries "greater emphasis on the need for speed" and "Hitting the new wing without delay is essential" W1. The crew's gybe cost per wind band is crew value.

8.5Gennaker rotation and sheet-load management

The gennaker of 38 m² S11 is the largest load on the boat. Aerodynamic force of apparent wind speed, so a small rise in apparent wind produces a much larger rise in sheet load, and because the apparent wind on a fast boat sailing downwind is lowest when boat speed is highest, the sheet is lightest when the boat is fastest. That inverse relation is the whole of sheet-load management: the crew keeps the boat fast, therefore the load low, therefore the boat controllable. The 49er crew is "looking to really over the gennaker from quite early on in the wind range" S29, and "Speed before depth is essential because once the boat is going well, the apparent wind goes forward and the boat can go lower" S27.

Rotation is the sail's shape turning about its luff. The manual warns: "Do not under trim the spinnaker as this will allow the leech to rotate up and twist around the luff resulting in a lack of power" W1. An undersheeted gennaker opens its leech, the head falls to leeward, drive is lost and, in breeze, the rotated leech and starts the windward roll. Oversheeting closes the leech, stalls the flow and heels the boat to leeward. The trimmer's reference is the luff curl, sheet eased until the luff just breaks and brought back on, and the hull attitude that stabilises the sail is the one specified for downwind: "The key is to sail the boat heeled to windward with the windward chine in the water" S12.

Through the gybe the sail rotates round the forestay. The old sheet is eased as the boat squares so the clew flies forward and the sail floats ahead of the boat; the new sheet is taken up as the sail blows through, and trimming it before the clew has passed the forestay hooks the sail on the jib and stops the rotation. The crew-first handling, "the old sheet in one hand and the new sheet in the other" W1, puts the rotation under one pair of hands. The loss is the interval between old-sheet release and new-sheet fill; on the new gybe the sheet comes on as the boat heads up so that load and speed rise together.

Sheet load is also the crew's signal. Rising load with rising speed is the boat working; rising load without rising speed is the boat overpowered, and the response is to bear away, which on a fast boat reduces apparent-wind speed, and to ease, rather than to ease alone, because a large ease lets the leech rotate and invites the windward roll. In extreme conditions the relation is inverted on purpose: "there's a risk of pitchpoling if you plough into the back of a wave at full pelt", so the gennaker is oversheeted to slow the boat S29. No published 49er polars were found; the target apparent-wind angle and sheet-load feel by wind band are the crew's own logged values, crew value.

8.6Bear-aways, gybe sets, and offset-mark manoeuvres

The bear-away at the top mark is the manoeuvre with the largest change of state. The manual sets the scene at 20 knots and above: boat speed into the mark is "probably 9-11 knots", and "a well executed bear away will take 5-7 seconds at the end of which the 49er will be moving at double the entry speed" W1. The reaching angles between close-hauled and the run are where the apparent wind is strongest and the mainsail leech loads the rudder most, and they are passed through fast.

The manual advises cracking off "a couple of degrees" before the mark for speed, and , "a few inches in moderate conditions and flat water or dump it completely in fresh, lumpy seas" W1. The rig section states the mechanism: "Release vang pressure considerably prior to bearing away. This will reduce mainsail leech and rudder loads" S12. With the vang on, the leech stays closed as the sheet is eased, the top of the mainsail keeps driving and heeling, the boat rounds up and the rudder fights it; with it off, the leech twists open and the turn needs a small rudder angle. Weight goes "right down to the back of the boat" with the hull flat, and the turn is positive: "It is almost impossible to do it too fast", whereas a slow arc produces "A massive and uncontrollable increase of force" W1. Once settled, the vang comes back on, since left too loose it can cause a nose-dive or W1.

The pitchpole is the limit. When the bow drives into the back of a wave the hull decelerates and the drive force high in the rig about its bow; in extreme conditions the gennaker is oversheeted to slow the boat S29. The hoist adds a state: in the windward set the crew moves in and starts the halyard while "The skipper stays out on the wing or on the wire and sails the boat flat bearing away", the boat is squared away by the first-spreader call and held "dead square/by the lee" until the head is about a metre from the tip, when the crew takes the new sheet W1. In the leeward set the helm stays out and holds speed, and the crew moves rapidly onto the wing with the sheet as the sail reaches the tip W1.

The gybe set is the same start "but bearaway process is continued into a gybe" W1. It is chosen when the strategy wants the headed gybe first, since the boat gybes on lifts to stay on the headed gybe S51, or to escape the dirty air of boats ahead S51. Its cost is a gybe before the gennaker has built speed, at the point of , so in strong wind it gives way to a bear-away set and a gybe once at speed. Where the course has an offset mark the bear-away is split: a partial turn onto the offset leg on two sails, then the hoist after the offset, with the zone of three hull lengths S1 deciding the inside position at both marks.

8.7Gate roundings and exit-speed control

The gate choice is the first strategic decision of the next beat, because each mark exits the boat onto a different tack and toward a different side. The criteria are Benjamin and Perry's: the mark farther upwind is closer, the least crowded mark is preferred, and the down-current mark turns faster S58; and Hunt's warning that a pinwheel at the right-hand gate mark can pin a boat indefinitely S59. The 49erFX study's finding that extra manoeuvres on the first and last legs were S37 suggests the extra gybe needed to reach the better mark is weighed against the gain rather than ruled out.

The drop sets the entry speed. In the windward drop the helm moves out onto the wire and takes the sheet; the crew moves off the wire into the boat, takes the slack out of the retrieving line and releases the halyard, while the helm bears away "quite sharply" for one to three seconds so the sail collapses behind the mainsail W1. The leeward drop is the same, with the sail safe once the patches are hard W1. The gybe drop keeps the old sheet "very tight", and once the boat has gybed and the sail has blown onto the rig the halyard is released while the old sheet is held W1. The timing is a balance: too early and the boat sails the rest of the leg at two-sail speed, too late and the crew is inboard as the load rises in the rounding. The crew's drop distance by wind band is crew value.

The rounding itself is wide then tight: the boat swings wide on the near side and cuts close on the far side, and is close-hauled before the bow reaches the mark S51. The manual adds: come away slightly on the approach to bring the boat to windward of the mark, keep the boat flat in the turn, keep "the jib well eased in the turn, then sheet on hard once the boat is on the breeze", because "If the boat is not flat before the turn, the heel will become exaggerated as the rig throws its out into the turn" W1. The rig's mass swings outward in the turn and adds to leeward heel; the eased jib lets the bow come up without stalling; the main comes in as the apparent wind moves forward. The exit standard is both sailors on the wire at close-hauled speed within a boat length of the mark, on the tack the gate choice was made for.

Mark-room governs the traffic. The three-hull-length zone and the overlap at the moment the first boat reaches it decide who must give room, and in reasonable doubt "it shall be presumed that she did not" obtain or break the overlap S1. An inside position is worth fighting for S51, but a boat that gains the inside with a slow, tight turn and a exit has paid more than it gained; the wide-then-tight geometry is available only to the boat that either has room or has left it.

8.8Loss-of-control recovery and capsize management

Loss of control has three physical modes, each with a warning signature. A leeward capsize occurs when the heeling moment exceeds the righting moment of crew weight over the S25; the warning is heel leaving the skiff coaching band in which "+/- 10 degrees is only just tolerable" S36. A occurs when the sail plan drives to windward with the crew's weight already out: the gennaker leech rotating up S12, the vang left too loose after a bear-away W1, or a sudden ease that removes the leeward force. A pitchpole occurs when the bow buries in the back of a wave S29, the hull decelerates, and the drive force high in the rig rotates the boat about its bow.

Recovery reverses the moment that is building, using the rudder while it still works. The foils generate lift only while moving through the water S35, so the helm steers first and the crew move second. Mast falling to leeward: bear away and ease. Mast falling to windward: head up and sheet on, bringing the apparent wind forward and the leeward force back. When the boat stops, the rudder has nothing and body weight is the only control; low trapezing S25.

The capsize sequence begins with the fall. The manual states that the sails "can not be expected to withstand the load of crews free falling from the wing height", that the load is spread by , and that the "Best escape is aft diving beyond the mainsail leech" W1. Once both sailors are accounted for, the gennaker is dealt with first: the halyard is freed and the sail drawn into the chute by the retrieval line, or at minimum both sheets are freed so the sail cannot fill as the rig rises. The mainsheet and vang are eased so the mainsail can weathervane. The boat is turned so the bow points into the wind and the mast lies downwind; with the mast to windward the wind gets under the rising sail and drops the boat back over onto the crew.

Righting is a moment balance. The crew's weight on the daggerboard acts through the lever of the board's extension, and the hull rises when that moment exceeds the moment of the 8.4 m mast S11 and the water in the sails. The helm holds the bow and swims the boat head-to-wind, then lies over the leeward wing to be scooped aboard as the hull comes up; the 2.9 m beam S9 means the rise ends fast, and the sailor on the board goes over the wing at once so the boat does not continue into a second capsize. An inverted boat is first brought to the horizontal by standing on the wing and hauling on the board. At the restart the jib is sheeted first to turn the bow down, the main is eased, speed comes before trim, and the gennaker is checked fully in the chute with the halyard clear. The time from capsize to full speed is the crew's own logged value, crew value.
